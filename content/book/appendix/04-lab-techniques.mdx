Science passages on the MCAT are full of experiments, and experiments are full of techniques. You won't be asked to run a Western blot, but you will be asked what a band on one means, why a researcher chose size-exclusion chromatography instead of ion exchange, or what an IR peak at 1715 cm⁻¹ tells you. For each technique, the key question is the same: **what property does it separate or measure by?** Once you know that, most technique questions become reasoning questions.

The tables below are grouped by type. Each one gives the technique, the property it depends on and the way it typically appears on the exam.

## Separations and Purification

### Extraction, distillation and other bulk methods

| Technique | Separates/measures by | Typical MCAT use |
|---|---|---|
| Liquid–liquid extraction | Relative solubility in two immiscible solvents, usually water and an organic solvent | Separating a polar or charged compound (aqueous layer) from a nonpolar one (organic layer) in a separatory funnel |
| Acid–base extraction | Converting a compound to its charged (water-soluble) form by protonating or deprotonating it | Adding a base (e.g., $\ce{NaHCO3}$ or $\ce{NaOH}$) pulls carboxylic acids (and, with strong base, phenols) into water; adding acid (e.g., $\ce{HCl}$) pulls amines into water |
| Simple distillation | Boiling point | Separating liquids whose boiling points differ by more than about 25 °C, or removing a volatile solvent from a nonvolatile solute |
| Fractional distillation | Boiling point, using a fractionating column for many vaporization–condensation cycles | Separating liquids with close boiling points |
| Vacuum distillation | Boiling point at reduced pressure | Compounds with high boiling points (above about 150 °C) or that decompose when heated; lowering pressure lowers the boiling point |
| Recrystallization | Difference in solubility at high and low temperature | Purifying a solid; the ideal solvent dissolves the compound when hot but not when cold |
| Filtration (gravity or vacuum) | Physical state (solid versus liquid) | Gravity filtration when you want the filtrate, often hot; vacuum filtration when you want the solid quickly |
| Sublimation | Ability to pass directly from solid to gas | Purifying solids that sublime, such as caffeine or naphthalene |
| Dialysis | Size, through a semipermeable membrane | Removing salts or small molecules from a protein solution |
| Salting out (e.g., ammonium sulfate precipitation) | Solubility at high salt concentration | Early, crude protein purification step |

<Callout type="trap" title="Which layer is which?">
The organic layer isn't always on top. Layers sort by **density**. Diethyl ether and ethyl acetate are less dense than water and float on it; dichloromethane and chloroform are denser and sink. Also, several small extractions with fresh solvent remove more of a compound than one large extraction with the same total volume.
</Callout>

### Chromatography

All chromatography separates compounds by how they distribute between a **stationary phase** and a **mobile phase**. A compound that interacts more strongly with the stationary phase moves more slowly.

| Technique | Separates/measures by | Typical MCAT use |
|---|---|---|
| Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) | Polarity. Usually a polar stationary phase (silica gel) and a less polar mobile phase | Monitoring a reaction's progress or checking purity. Less polar compounds travel farther and have higher $R_f$ values. $R_f$ = distance traveled by the spot ÷ distance traveled by the solvent front |
| Column chromatography | Polarity (same principle as TLC, on a larger scale) | Purifying a reaction product; on a polar column, nonpolar compounds elute first |
| High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) | Polarity, under high pressure with fine particles for better resolution. **Reverse-phase** HPLC uses a nonpolar stationary phase, so polar compounds elute first | Separating and quantifying compounds in a mixture, such as a drug and its metabolites in a blood sample |
| Gas chromatography (GC) | Volatility (boiling point) and interaction with the stationary phase; the mobile phase is an inert gas, such as helium | Separating volatile compounds; often coupled with mass spectrometry (GC–MS) to identify them. Lower-boiling compounds usually elute first |
| Size-exclusion chromatography (gel filtration) | Size. Beads contain pores that small molecules enter, which slows them | Separating proteins by size under native conditions; **large molecules elute first** |
| Ion-exchange chromatography | Charge. Cation-exchange beads are negatively charged and bind positive molecules; anion-exchange beads are positively charged and bind negative molecules | Separating proteins by charge at a chosen pH; bound proteins are eluted with increasing salt or a pH change |
| Affinity chromatography | Specific binding to a ligand attached to the beads, such as an antibody, a substrate analog or nickel ions for His-tagged proteins | Purifying one specific protein in a single step; eluted with excess free ligand or a competitor (e.g., imidazole for His tags) |

<Callout type="high-yield" title="Size exclusion runs backward">
In gel electrophoresis, small molecules move fastest. In size-exclusion chromatography, **large** molecules come off the column first, because they can't enter the beads' pores and take the shortest path through the column. The MCAT tests this reversal often.
</Callout>

## Spectroscopy

| Technique | Separates/measures by | Typical MCAT use |
|---|---|---|
| Infrared (IR) spectroscopy | Absorption of IR light by vibrating bonds; each functional group absorbs at characteristic wavenumbers | Identifying functional groups, such as confirming that an alcohol was oxidized to a ketone (O–H peak lost, C=O peak gained) |
| Ultraviolet–visible (UV-Vis) spectroscopy | Absorption of UV or visible light by electronic transitions, especially in conjugated π systems | Measuring concentration (Beer–Lambert law, $A = \varepsilon b c$); detecting conjugation; more conjugation shifts absorbance to longer wavelengths |
| Proton nuclear magnetic resonance (¹H-NMR) | Absorption of radio waves by hydrogen nuclei in a magnetic field; depends on each hydrogen's chemical environment | Determining structure: number of hydrogen environments, their electronic environment, neighboring hydrogens and relative counts |
| Mass spectrometry (MS) | Mass-to-charge ratio ($m/z$) of ionized molecules and fragments | Finding molecular mass and identifying compounds from fragments; isotope patterns |

### IR: key absorptions

| Bond or group | Wavenumber (cm⁻¹) | Appearance |
|---|---|---|
| O–H (alcohol) | 3200–3550 | Strong, **broad** (hydrogen bonding) |
| O–H (carboxylic acid) | 2500–3300 | Very broad, often overlapping the C–H region |
| N–H (amine, amide) | 3300–3500 | Medium, sharper than O–H; primary amines show two peaks, secondary amines one |
| C–H (sp³) | 2850–3000 | Just below 3000 |
| C–H (sp², alkene or aromatic) | 3000–3100 | Just above 3000 |
| C–H (sp, terminal alkyne) | About 3300 | Strong, sharp |
| C–H (aldehyde) | About 2720 and 2820 | Two weak peaks |
| C≡N (nitrile) | 2210–2260 | Medium, sharp |
| C≡C (alkyne) | 2100–2260 | Weak to medium |
| C=O (carbonyl) | 1650–1780; about 1700–1750 for most ketones, aldehydes, esters and acids | **Strong, sharp**: the most recognizable peak. Esters toward the high end (about 1735–1750), amides lower (about 1650–1690) |
| C=C (alkene) | 1620–1680 | Medium to weak |
| C=C (aromatic ring) | About 1450–1600 | Several medium peaks |
| C–O (alcohol, ether, ester) | 1000–1300 | Strong |

The region below about 1500 cm⁻¹ is the **fingerprint region**. It is unique to each molecule but rarely interpreted peak by peak on the MCAT.

### ¹H-NMR: typical chemical shifts

Shifts are measured in ppm relative to tetramethylsilane (TMS) at 0 ppm. Electronegative atoms and π systems pull electron density away from a hydrogen (**deshielding** it), which moves its signal **downfield**, to higher ppm.

| Type of hydrogen | Approximate shift (ppm) |
|---|---|
| Alkyl (C–H on sp³ carbon) | 0.9–1.5 |
| Next to a C=O or C=C (allylic, α to carbonyl), or on a benzylic carbon | 2.0–2.5 |
| Terminal alkyne (≡C–H) | About 2–3 |
| On a carbon bonded to O, N or a halogen (e.g., –CH₂–O–) | 3.3–4.5 |
| Vinyl (C=C–H) | 4.5–6.5 |
| Aromatic (Ar–H) | 6.5–8.0 |
| Aldehyde (–CHO) | 9–10 |
| Carboxylic acid (–COOH) | 10.5–12, broad |
| Alcohol O–H and amine N–H | Variable (about 1–5), often broad |

| NMR feature | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Number of signals | Number of chemically distinct hydrogen environments |
| Chemical shift | The electronic environment of each set of hydrogens |
| Integration (area under the peak) | The relative number of hydrogens producing each signal |
| Splitting (multiplicity) | The number of hydrogens on adjacent carbons, by the $n + 1$ rule: $n$ neighbors split a signal into $n + 1$ peaks |

<Callout type="note" title="The ethyl pattern">
An ethyl group ($\ce{-CH2CH3}$) appears as a quartet integrating to 2H (split by the three CH₃ hydrogens) and a triplet integrating to 3H (split by the two CH₂ hydrogens). An isopropyl group appears as a septet (1H) and a doublet (6H). Recognizing these two patterns quickly settles many structure questions.
</Callout>

### UV-Vis and mass spectrometry details

| Feature | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Absorbance at 280 nm | Protein concentration, mainly from tryptophan and tyrosine |
| Absorbance at 260 nm | Nucleic acid concentration, from the bases |
| $A_{260}/A_{280}$ ratio | Nucleic acid purity; about 1.8 for pure DNA and about 2.0 for pure RNA; lower values suggest protein contamination |
| Color of a compound | It absorbs the complementary color; a compound absorbing in the orange–red range appears blue–green |
| Molecular ion peak ($\text{M}^+$) | The molecular mass of the compound (for a +1 ion, $m/z$ equals the mass) |
| Base peak | The most abundant ion, set to 100% relative abundance; not necessarily the molecular ion |
| $\text{M} + 2$ peak about one-third the height of $\text{M}^+$ | One chlorine atom ($\ce{^{35}Cl}$ : $\ce{^{37}Cl}$ is about 3 : 1) |
| $\text{M} + 2$ peak about equal in height to $\text{M}^+$ | One bromine atom ($\ce{^{79}Br}$ : $\ce{^{81}Br}$ is about 1 : 1) |

## Molecular Biology

| Technique | Separates/measures by | Typical MCAT use |
|---|---|---|
| Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) | Amplifies a specific DNA sequence defined by two primers, through repeated cycles of denaturation (about 95 °C), primer annealing (about 50–65 °C) and extension by a heat-stable polymerase such as *Taq* (about 72 °C) | Amplifying DNA for detection, cloning or sequencing; each cycle roughly doubles the target, so $n$ cycles give up to $2^n$ copies |
| Reverse-transcription PCR (RT-PCR) | Converts RNA to complementary DNA (cDNA) with reverse transcriptase, then amplifies it | Detecting or measuring gene expression (mRNA), or detecting RNA viruses |
| Quantitative PCR (qPCR, real-time PCR) | Fluorescence measured each cycle; fewer cycles to reach a threshold means more starting template | Comparing the amount of a DNA or mRNA between samples |
| Agarose gel electrophoresis | Size of DNA or RNA fragments. Nucleic acids are negatively charged (phosphate backbone) and migrate toward the positive electrode (anode); smaller fragments move farther | Checking PCR products, restriction digests, DNA fingerprinting |
| SDS-PAGE | Protein **mass**. SDS denatures proteins and coats them with negative charge roughly in proportion to their length, so charge and shape no longer matter. Adding a reducing agent (β-mercaptoethanol or DTT) also breaks disulfide bonds | Estimating a protein's molecular weight; comparing reducing and nonreducing gels reveals subunits linked by disulfide bonds |
| Native PAGE | Size, shape and charge of folded proteins | Studying proteins in their native, active form or their complexes |
| Isoelectric focusing | Isoelectric point (pI). Proteins migrate through a pH gradient until they reach the pH where their net charge is zero | Separating proteins by charge; combined with SDS-PAGE in two-dimensional gel electrophoresis |
| Southern blot | Detects a specific **DNA** sequence, using a labeled complementary probe after electrophoresis | Detecting a gene, a deletion or a restriction fragment length polymorphism |
| Northern blot | Detects a specific **RNA**, using a labeled complementary probe | Measuring which tissues express a gene and how much mRNA they make |
| Western blot | Detects a specific **protein**, using antibodies after SDS-PAGE | Confirming a protein's presence, size and relative amount; checking a knockout or knockdown |
| ELISA (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay) | Antibody binding to an antigen, reported by an enzyme that produces a color or light signal | Measuring the concentration of a protein (such as a hormone or cytokine) or detecting antibodies in serum |
| Sanger (dideoxy) sequencing | DNA synthesis with a small amount of dideoxynucleotides (ddNTPs), which lack a 3′-OH and end the chain; fragments are separated by size to read the sequence | Determining a DNA sequence one fragment at a time |
| Next-generation (high-throughput) sequencing | Massively parallel sequencing of many short fragments at once | Sequencing genomes or transcriptomes (RNA-seq) |
| DNA microarray | Hybridization of labeled cDNA to thousands of known probes on a chip | Comparing the expression of many genes at once |
| Molecular cloning | Restriction enzymes cut DNA at specific (often palindromic) sites; DNA ligase joins the insert into a plasmid vector carrying an origin of replication and a selectable marker (such as antibiotic resistance); bacteria are transformed and selected | Producing many copies of a gene or expressing a protein in bacteria |
| cDNA library | mRNA reverse-transcribed into cDNA, which lacks introns | Expressing eukaryotic genes in bacteria, which can't remove introns |
| CRISPR–Cas9 | A guide RNA directs the Cas9 nuclease to a matching DNA sequence next to a short PAM site, where it makes a double-strand break | Gene editing. Error-prone repair by nonhomologous end joining causes small insertions or deletions that knock out a gene; homology-directed repair with a supplied template makes a precise change |
| RNA interference (siRNA, shRNA) | Short RNAs direct degradation or silencing of a complementary mRNA | Knocking down a gene's expression without changing its DNA |
| Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) | A fluorescent probe binds a complementary sequence in cells or chromosomes | Locating a gene on a chromosome; detecting gene amplification or translocations |

<Callout type="mnemonic" title="SNoW DRoP">
**S**outhern = **D**NA, **No**rthern = **R**NA, **W**estern = **P**rotein. (Read across: S–D, No–R, W–P.)
</Callout>

<Callout type="trap" title="Gel direction">
DNA runs toward the **positive** electrode, because its backbone is negative. Remember that the electrophoresis chamber is an electrolytic cell: the anode is positive there, unlike in a galvanic cell. "DNA runs to red" (the positive lead) is a common lab reminder.
</Callout>

<Callout type="connection" title="Where proteins come from" to="/bb/amino-acids-and-proteins">
Isoelectric focusing, ion exchange and SDS-PAGE all depend on amino-acid side chains: their charge at a given pH and the length of the chain. The amino acids chapter explains how to work out a protein's charge.
</Callout>

## Cell and Protein Techniques

| Technique | Separates/measures by | Typical MCAT use |
|---|---|---|
| Differential centrifugation | Size and density; spinning at progressively higher speeds pellets progressively smaller components | Separating organelles: nuclei pellet first at low speed, then mitochondria (and chloroplasts or lysosomes), then microsomes (ER and membrane fragments), then ribosomes at the highest speeds |
| Density-gradient centrifugation | Buoyant density in a sucrose or cesium chloride gradient; each component settles where its density matches the gradient's | Separating macromolecules by density, as in the Meselson–Stahl experiment showing semiconservative DNA replication |
| Flow cytometry | Light scattering and fluorescence of single cells passing a laser one at a time. Forward scatter reflects size; side scatter reflects internal complexity (granularity); fluorescent antibodies identify surface markers | Counting and characterizing cell populations, such as CD4⁺ T cells in HIV infection; FACS (fluorescence-activated cell sorting) physically sorts cells |
| Bright-field light microscopy | Contrast from light absorbed by stained samples | Viewing stained tissue, Gram-stained bacteria; resolution limit about 0.2 μm |
| Phase-contrast microscopy | Differences in refractive index converted into contrast | Viewing living, unstained cells |
| Fluorescence and confocal microscopy | Emission from fluorescent dyes or proteins (such as GFP); confocal microscopy removes out-of-focus light to make sharp optical sections | Locating a protein within a cell; tracking proteins in living cells |
| Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) | Electrons transmitted through a very thin section | Internal ultrastructure of organelles; far higher resolution than light microscopy; samples are fixed (not living) |
| Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) | Electrons scattered from a metal-coated surface | Detailed three-dimensional images of surfaces |
| Cryo-electron microscopy | Electron images of rapidly frozen samples, averaged over many particles | Determining structures of large proteins and complexes without crystallizing them |
| X-ray crystallography | Diffraction of X-rays by a crystal | Determining high-resolution three-dimensional protein structures |
| Bradford assay | Color change when Coomassie dye binds protein, measured by absorbance | Measuring total protein concentration against a standard curve |
| Immunoprecipitation (IP) and co-IP | Antibody binding pulls a specific protein, and any proteins bound to it, out of a mixture | Showing that two proteins interact in cells |
| Edman degradation | Sequential removal and identification of the N-terminal amino acid | Determining a short peptide's sequence from the N-terminus |
| Patch clamp | Current through ion channels in a small patch of membrane | Measuring ion-channel activity and membrane potentials in neurons |

<Callout type="note" title="Reading a technique question">
When a passage introduces an unfamiliar method, ask three questions. What property does it depend on: size, charge, polarity, binding or mass? What would the result look like if the hypothesis were true? And what is the control? Those three questions answer most technique items, even for methods you have never seen before.
</Callout>
