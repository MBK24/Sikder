The MCAT expects you to know all 20 standard amino acids: their names, both abbreviations, their side-chain classes and the handful of special properties that show up repeatedly in passages. This page collects that information in one place. Use it as a reference while you study, and use the flashcard deck to drill it.

You don't need to memorize every pKa to two decimal places. You do need to know which side chains are ionizable, roughly where their pKa values fall, and what charge each amino acid carries at physiological pH (about 7.4).

## The 20 Standard Amino Acids

Side-chain pKa values are given only for side chains that are commonly treated as ionizable. Published values vary by a few tenths depending on the source and conditions, so treat them as approximate. The isoelectric point (pI) values are approximate for the free amino acid.

| Amino acid | 3-letter | 1-letter | Side-chain class | Side-chain pKa | Approx. pI | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Glycine | Gly | G | Nonpolar, aliphatic | — | 6.0 | Side chain is a single H; the only **achiral** amino acid; very flexible, so it disrupts α-helices; common in collagen and tight turns |
| Alanine | Ala | A | Nonpolar, aliphatic | — | 6.0 | Methyl side chain; strong helix former; produced from pyruvate by transamination |
| Valine | Val | V | Nonpolar, aliphatic | — | 6.0 | **Essential**; branched-chain amino acid; the Glu→Val substitution in β-globin causes sickle-cell disease |
| Leucine | Leu | L | Nonpolar, aliphatic | — | 6.0 | **Essential**; branched-chain; purely **ketogenic** |
| Isoleucine | Ile | I | Nonpolar, aliphatic | — | 6.0 | **Essential**; branched-chain; two chiral centers; both glucogenic and ketogenic |
| Methionine | Met | M | Nonpolar, aliphatic | — | 5.7 | **Essential**; **sulfur-containing** (thioether, no disulfides); encoded by AUG, the start codon; precursor of S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), the main methyl donor |
| Proline | Pro | P | Nonpolar, aliphatic | — | 6.5 | Side chain loops back to the backbone nitrogen, forming a ring (a secondary amine); rigid **helix breaker**; common in collagen and β-turns |
| Phenylalanine | Phe | F | Aromatic | — | 5.5 | **Essential**; benzyl side chain, very hydrophobic; converted to tyrosine (blocked in phenylketonuria) |
| Tyrosine | Tyr | Y | Aromatic | ≈ 10.1 | 5.7 | Phenolic –OH, so more polar than Phe; **phosphorylation** site; precursor of catecholamines, thyroid hormones and melanin; absorbs at 280 nm; conditionally essential (made from Phe) |
| Tryptophan | Trp | W | Aromatic | — | 5.9 | **Essential**; bulky indole ring; largest side chain; strongest 280 nm absorber; precursor of serotonin, melatonin and niacin |
| Serine | Ser | S | Polar, uncharged | — | 5.7 | Hydroxyl side chain; **phosphorylation** site; nucleophile in serine proteases; O-linked glycosylation site |
| Threonine | Thr | T | Polar, uncharged | — | 5.9 | **Essential**; hydroxyl side chain; two chiral centers; phosphorylation and O-linked glycosylation site |
| Cysteine | Cys | C | Polar, uncharged | ≈ 8.3 | 5.1 | **Sulfur-containing** thiol; oxidizes to form **disulfide bonds** (cystine) that stabilize tertiary and quaternary structure; the only standard amino acid with R configuration at its α-carbon |
| Asparagine | Asn | N | Polar, uncharged | — | 5.4 | Amide of aspartate; site of **N-linked glycosylation** |
| Glutamine | Gln | Q | Polar, uncharged | — | 5.7 | Amide of glutamate; major carrier of nitrogen in the blood |
| Aspartate (aspartic acid) | Asp | D | Acidic (negatively charged) | ≈ 3.7 | 2.8 | Negative at pH 7.4; active-site residue in many enzymes; amino group donor in the urea cycle |
| Glutamate (glutamic acid) | Glu | E | Acidic (negatively charged) | ≈ 4.2 | 3.2 | Negative at pH 7.4; main excitatory neurotransmitter; precursor of GABA |
| Lysine | Lys | K | Basic (positively charged) | ≈ 10.5 | 9.7 | **Essential**; positive at pH 7.4; purely **ketogenic**; binds DNA in histones; acetylation and ubiquitination site |
| Arginine | Arg | R | Basic (positively charged) | ≈ 12.5 | 10.8 | Positive at pH 7.4; guanidinium group, the most basic side chain; conditionally essential (essential in growth); precursor of nitric oxide; urea-cycle intermediate |
| Histidine | His | H | Basic (positively charged) | ≈ 6.0 | 7.6 | **Essential**; imidazole ring; side-chain pKa near physiological pH, so it can gain or lose a proton easily; acid–base catalyst in active sites and an important buffer (in hemoglobin, for example); **mostly uncharged** at pH 7.4 |

Backbone groups: the α-carboxyl group has a pKa of about 2 (roughly 1.8–2.4), and the α-amino group has a pKa of about 9–10 (roughly 9.0–10.5). At physiological pH, a free amino acid exists mainly as a **zwitterion**, with $\ce{-COO-}$ and $\ce{-NH3+}$.

<Callout type="note" title="Classification varies by textbook">
This table uses five classes: nonpolar aliphatic, aromatic, polar uncharged, acidic and basic. Some resources group tyrosine with the polar amino acids and phenylalanine and tryptophan with the nonpolar ones. Others class cysteine as nonpolar, because its thiol is only weakly polar. If a passage defines a classification, use the passage's. Histidine's ring is also aromatic, but it is almost always grouped with the basic amino acids.
</Callout>

### Groups worth knowing

| Group | Members | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Essential (must come from the diet) | His, Ile, Leu, Lys, Met, Phe, Thr, Trp, Val | Nutrition and deficiency questions |
| Conditionally essential | Arg, Cys, Tyr (among others) | Needed in the diet in some conditions, such as growth, illness or blocked synthesis (Tyr in phenylketonuria) |
| Sulfur-containing | Cys, Met | Only Cys forms disulfide bonds |
| Aromatic | Phe, Tyr, Trp (His also has an aromatic ring) | UV absorbance at 280 nm (mostly Trp and Tyr) |
| Hydroxyl-containing | Ser, Thr, Tyr | Phosphorylation by kinases; O-linked glycosylation (Ser, Thr) |
| Amide-containing | Asn, Gln | Not acidic, despite being derived from Asp and Glu |
| Branched-chain | Val, Leu, Ile | Metabolized mainly in muscle; maple syrup urine disease |
| Charged at pH 7.4 | Asp, Glu (negative); Lys, Arg (positive) | Salt bridges, solubility, electrophoresis; His is mostly neutral at 7.4 |
| Helix breakers | Pro, Gly | Pro is rigid and lacks an N–H to donate; Gly is too flexible |
| Two chiral centers | Ile, Thr | Stereochemistry questions |
| Purely ketogenic | Leu, Lys | Can't be converted to glucose |

<Callout type="trap" title="Asparagine and glutamine are not acidic">
Students often lump Asn and Gln with Asp and Glu because the names look alike. Asn and Gln carry an amide, which is neither acidic nor basic under physiological conditions. Their side chains are polar but uncharged.
</Callout>

## Calculating the Isoelectric Point

The **isoelectric point (pI)** is the pH at which a molecule carries no net charge. At a pH below the pI, an amino acid is net positive; above it, net negative. In electrophoresis, a molecule doesn't migrate at its pI. That's the principle behind isoelectric focusing.

To find the pI:

1. Write out the pKa values for every ionizable group.
2. Identify the two pKa values that bracket the neutral (zero net charge) form.
3. Average them.

In practice, this gives three cases:

| Type of amino acid | Which pKa values to average | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Neutral side chain (no ionizable R group) | α-COOH and α-NH₃⁺ | Glycine: $\text{pI} = \dfrac{2.3 + 9.6}{2} \approx 6.0$ |
| Acidic side chain (Asp, Glu) | α-COOH and side-chain COOH (the two **lowest**) | Aspartate: $\text{pI} = \dfrac{1.9 + 3.7}{2} \approx 2.8$ |
| Basic side chain (Lys, Arg, His) | Side chain and α-NH₃⁺ (the two **highest**) | Lysine: $\text{pI} = \dfrac{9.0 + 10.5}{2} \approx 9.7$ |

### Worked example: histidine

Histidine has three ionizable groups: α-COOH (pKa about 1.8), the imidazole side chain (about 6.0) and α-NH₃⁺ (about 9.2). Going from very low pH to very high pH:

| pH range | α-COOH | Imidazole | α-NH₃⁺ | Net charge |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Below 1.8 | COOH (0) | Protonated (+1) | NH₃⁺ (+1) | +2 |
| 1.8 to 6.0 | COO⁻ (−1) | Protonated (+1) | NH₃⁺ (+1) | +1 |
| 6.0 to 9.2 | COO⁻ (−1) | Neutral (0) | NH₃⁺ (+1) | **0** |
| Above 9.2 | COO⁻ (−1) | Neutral (0) | NH₂ (0) | −1 |

The neutral form exists between pKa values of 6.0 and 9.2, so $\text{pI} \approx \dfrac{6.0 + 9.2}{2} = 7.6$.

### Charge at a given pH

For any ionizable group, compare the pH with the group's pKa:

- **pH below pKa:** the group is mostly protonated. For a carboxyl, that means neutral COOH; for an amine, positive NH₃⁺.
- **pH above pKa:** the group is mostly deprotonated. For a carboxyl, negative COO⁻; for an amine, neutral NH₂.
- **pH equal to pKa:** the group is half protonated, by the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation.

<Callout type="high-yield" title="A peptide's charge is the sum of its groups">
For a peptide, only the N-terminal amino group, the C-terminal carboxyl group and the ionizable side chains matter. Internal backbone groups are tied up in peptide bonds. To find net charge at a given pH, assign each group's charge using the rule above and add them up.
</Callout>

## Memorization Tips

You will need this table in fast, reliable recall, not just recognition. A few approaches help.

### Learn by group, not alphabetically

Learn the amino acids in their classes, as in the table above. Classes carry meaning: knowing that Lys is basic tells you its charge, where it sits in a protein (usually on the surface) and how it behaves in electrophoresis, all at once.

### Learn structure and abbreviation together

Draw each side chain from memory while saying its name and both abbreviations. Most abbreviations are the first letter or first three letters of the name. The ones that aren't are worth learning on purpose:

| 1-letter code | Amino acid | One way to remember it |
|---|---|---|
| F | Phenylalanine | "**F**enylalanine" (the "ph" sounds like F) |
| Y | Tyrosine | "t**Y**rosine," the second letter |
| W | Tryptophan | The indole's double ring looks like a **W** |
| R | Arginine | "a**R**ginine," the sound of the first syllable |
| K | Lysine | K comes just before L, and L was taken |
| D | Aspartate | "aspar**D**ic," with the "t" softened |
| E | Glutamate | "glu**E**tamate"; E is after D, as Glu has one more carbon than Asp |
| N | Asparagine | "asparagi**N**e" |
| Q | Glutamine | "**Q**-tamine" |

<Callout type="mnemonic" title="The nine essential amino acids">
A popular device is **PVT TIM HaLL**: **P**he, **V**al, **T**rp, **T**hr, **I**le, **M**et, **H**is, **L**eu, **L**ys. Arginine is sometimes added as conditionally essential.
</Callout>

### Practice actively

- Use the flashcard deck for this page daily during content review, until you can name, abbreviate and classify every amino acid without hesitating.
- Draw the full table from memory once a week and check it against this page.
- Practice reading short peptide sequences in one-letter code (for example, MKD or HWY) and stating each residue's class and charge at pH 7.4.
