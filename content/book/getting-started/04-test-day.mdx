By test day, the learning is done. What remains is execution: arriving rested, moving through the day without surprises and giving each section your full attention. That's easier when nothing about the day is new to you. This chapter walks through the final week, the day before and the day itself, so that by the time you sit down for the first section, the logistics are routine.

Procedures at testing centers are set by the AAMC and the testing-center operator, and they are updated from time to time. We describe the day in general terms. For exact rules about identification, permitted items, breaks and rescheduling, read the AAMC's current essentials guide for MCAT examinees before your test date. Read it again the week of your exam.

## The Week Before

The last week is for consolidation, not cramming. You can't learn large new topics in a week, but you can make what you know more reliable and arrive rested.

- **Take your last full-length practice exam about a week out**, then review it thoroughly. Taking another full-length exam in the final two or three days usually costs more in fatigue than it gains in information.
- **Shift to lighter review.** Work through your error log, your flashcards and the High-Yield and Common Trap boxes in the chapters you found hardest. Keep sessions shorter than usual.
- **Fix your sleep schedule.** If your exam starts early in the morning, start waking up at that time now, not the night before. A sleep schedule takes several days to shift.
- **Do a dry run.** If you can, travel to the testing center at the time of day you'll travel on test day. Note how long it takes, where to park or which stop to get off at, and which entrance to use.
- **Confirm the details.** Check your appointment time and testing-center address in your AAMC account. Check that your ID is valid and that the name on it exactly matches the name on your registration.

## The Day Before

Treat the day before as a recovery day.

- **Study very little, or not at all.** A short skim of a few flashcards is fine if it calms you. A long study session won't help and may raise your anxiety.
- **Pack your bag** (see the checklist below) and set it by the door.
- **Plan your meals and snacks.** Eat foods you know agree with you. Test day is not the day to try something new.
- **Do something pleasant and low-key**: a walk, a film, dinner with a friend.
- **Go to bed at your normal time.** Don't go to bed hours early, which often just means lying awake. One imperfect night of sleep is common and rarely decisive. The sleep you got over the past week matters more.

## What to Bring

### Identification

You must present acceptable identification to be admitted. In general terms, it must be a current, government-issued photo ID with your signature, and the name on it must match the name on your MCAT registration. The AAMC defines exactly which documents are acceptable and what happens if there is a problem, so check its current ID requirements well in advance. If your ID expires before test day, or your name has changed, deal with it weeks ahead, not the night before.

<Callout type="high-yield" title="Check your ID now">
ID problems are one of the few things that can stop you from testing at all, and they are completely preventable. Compare the name on your ID with the name on your registration, letter by letter, and check the expiration date. Do this as soon as you register, and again the week before.
</Callout>

### A test-day checklist

| Bring | Why |
|---|---|
| Acceptable ID | Required for admission (check AAMC rules) |
| Snacks and a drink | For the breaks. Choose something that gives steady energy, such as nuts, a sandwich, fruit or a granola bar, plus water or your usual drink. |
| Layers of clothing | Testing rooms can be cold or warm. Check the AAMC's rules on what clothing is permitted in the testing room. |
| Any approved medication or comfort items | Bring only what the rules allow; arrange any accommodations through the AAMC well in advance. |
| Directions and appointment details | So that you don't depend on your phone at the last minute |

Your personal belongings, including your phone and watch, are stored outside the testing room, typically in a locker. During breaks you can usually get to your snacks and drink, but the rules limit what else you may use, and phones and other electronic devices are generally off-limits for the whole session, breaks included. You won't be allowed to bring your own scratch paper or calculator. The testing center provides a booklet and marker for notes. There is no calculator on the MCAT, so any arithmetic is designed to be done by hand or by estimation.

## Check-In

Plan to arrive early. The AAMC recommends arriving well before your scheduled time, because check-in involves several security steps and late arrival can mean you aren't admitted.

Check-in typically includes having your ID inspected, having your photo taken, providing a signature and completing a biometric identity check. You'll store your belongings and may be asked to show that your pockets are empty. The same identity checks are often repeated each time you leave and re-enter the testing room. This is routine; don't let it rattle you.

Once you're seated, there are a few short, untimed or separately timed steps before the first section: an examinee agreement and an optional tutorial on the test interface. If you have practiced with the official AAMC practice exams, the interface will already be familiar, and you can skip most or all of the tutorial. Use any spare minutes to settle, breathe and write down anything you want on your noteboard, such as a pacing plan.

## How the Day Runs

The day follows a fixed sequence. The four scored sections always come in the same order, with optional breaks between them.

| Segment | Length | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Check-in, agreement, optional tutorial | Varies | Security steps, then short introductory screens |
| Chemical and Physical Foundations | 95 min | 59 questions |
| Optional break | Short | |
| Critical Analysis and Reasoning Skills | 90 min | 53 questions |
| Optional break | Longer, mid-exam | Eat a proper snack here |
| Biological and Biochemical Foundations | 95 min | 59 questions |
| Optional break | Short | |
| Psychological, Social, and Biological Foundations | 95 min | 59 questions |
| Void-or-score decision, then optional survey | Short | |

From arrival to departure, the whole day typically comes to about seven and a half hours of seated time, plus check-in.

### Breaks

There are optional breaks between sections: a shorter one after the first section, a longer one in the middle of the exam after CARS, and another shorter one after the third section. The AAMC sets their exact lengths. The breaks are optional, but you should take every one of them. Even a few minutes away from the screen helps you start the next section fresh.

Keep careful track of the time during breaks. The exam clock keeps running, and if you return late, the extra time can come out of the next section. Know how long it takes to get through re-entry checks, and head back with a margin.

A good break routine:

1. Use the restroom, even if you don't feel you need to.
2. Eat and drink something. The longer mid-exam break is the time for your most substantial snack.
3. Move: stretch, walk around, roll your shoulders.
4. Don't review notes or think about the section you just finished. It's done.

## Pacing Each Section

Pacing is easier when you know your numbers in advance.

| Section | Questions | Time | Average per question | A useful checkpoint |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C/P | 59 | 95 min | About 1.6 min | About halfway through the questions with about 47 minutes left |
| CARS | 53 | 90 min | About 1.7 min | About 10 minutes per passage, including its questions |
| B/B | 59 | 95 min | About 1.6 min | About halfway through the questions with about 47 minutes left |
| P/S | 59 | 95 min | About 1.6 min | About halfway through the questions with about 47 minutes left |

In the science sections, a passage and its questions together will often take around 7–8 minutes, while discrete questions usually take less than the average, which gives you back time. Some passages are harder than others. Average pace matters; perfect pace on every passage doesn't.

A few pacing principles hold for every section:

- **Don't let one question take your time.** If you are stuck after a reasonable effort, eliminate what you can, choose your best guess, flag it, and move on. There is no penalty for wrong answers, so never leave a question blank.
- **Check your pace at a couple of planned points**, not after every question. Constant clock-checking costs attention.
- **Leave a little time at the end** to answer any blank questions and revisit flagged ones.
- **Answer everything before time runs out**, even if the last few are educated guesses.

<Callout type="trap" title="Saving the hard passage for later">
Skipping a hard passage to come back to it at the end sounds efficient, but it often backfires. Students lose time navigating, forget what they read and then rush the passage at the end. If you do skip, have a firm rule for when you'll return, and don't skip more than one passage per section.
</Callout>

## Managing Anxiety

Some nerves are normal and even helpful. They keep you alert. The goal is not to feel calm but to stay functional while feeling nervous.

Strategies with good evidence behind them include:

- **Slow breathing.** A few slow breaths with a longer exhale than inhale helps activate the parasympathetic nervous system and reduces the physical symptoms of anxiety. Practice this during your full-length exams so that it is automatic on the day.
- **Reappraisal.** Telling yourself "this feeling is my body getting ready to perform" tends to work better than trying to suppress the feeling.
- **A pre-section routine.** Do the same thing at the start of every section: settle in your seat, take two slow breaths, read the first passage's opening sentence. A familiar routine lowers the sense of threat.
- **Focusing on the current question.** Anxiety lives in the future ("what if I fail?") and the past ("I definitely missed that one"). The question in front of you is the only thing you can affect.

If you have a diagnosed condition that affects testing, such as an anxiety disorder or ADHD, look into testing accommodations through the AAMC well in advance. The application process can take time.

## When a Section Goes Badly

At some point during the day, you will probably feel that a section went poorly. Most students do. That feeling is a much less reliable guide than it seems:

- **You remember the hard questions.** The ones you struggled with are vivid; the many you answered easily fade.
- **A hard form doesn't necessarily mean a low score.** Equating adjusts for differences in difficulty between forms (see [How the MCAT Is Scored](/getting-started/how-the-mcat-is-scored)).
- **Unscored questions** may have been among the ones that threw you.

So treat each section as a fresh start. During the break, do your break routine and deliberately set the previous section aside. Each section is scored separately, and an excellent remaining three sections can carry a disappointing one.

<Callout type="note" title="A reset you can use in the moment">
If you feel panic building mid-section, stop for 15 seconds. Take your hands off the mouse, take two slow breaths, and then reread the current question from the beginning. Those seconds are almost always recovered by reading more accurately afterward.
</Callout>

## Voiding or Scoring

After the last section, you'll be asked whether to have your exam scored or voided. You'll have a limited amount of time to decide.

- If you choose to **score** the exam, it will be scored and the results released on the published schedule.
- If you choose to **void** it, it won't be scored, and medical schools won't receive a score for it. A voided exam still counts toward the AAMC's attempt limits.

The decision is generally final once made, so read the screen carefully. Check the AAMC's current policy for details, including what happens if you don't make a choice in time.

### How to decide

Decide in advance, not in the moment. Before test day, write down the specific circumstances under which you would void. Good reasons are concrete and serious: you were acutely ill, a serious disruption at the testing center took significant time from a section, or you were unable to finish a section by a wide margin.

"I felt like a section went badly" is usually *not* a good reason to void. For the reasons above, that feeling is a poor predictor of your score. Many students who were sure they'd done badly end up with scores close to their practice average. Voiding throws that outcome away, still uses one of your attempts and delays your application. If none of your pre-written conditions apply, choose to score the exam.

## After the Exam

Collect your belongings, leave and rest. Eat something good. Spend time with people you like. Don't try to reconstruct questions from memory or comb online forums for answers: it won't change your score, and it's likely to cost you a lot of sleep. Testing rules also prohibit sharing exam content.

Your scores will arrive on the AAMC's published release date. In the meantime, get back to the rest of your application, or simply enjoy having the MCAT behind you.

## Summary

- In the final week, take your last full-length exam about a week out, shift your sleep to match your exam start time, do a dry run of the trip and confirm your ID matches your registration.
- Make the day before a rest day, with your bag packed.
- Arrive early. Check-in includes ID and biometric checks, and personal items stay in a locker.
- Take every optional break, eat during the mid-exam one, and get back on time.
- Pace at about 1.6 minutes per question in the science sections and about 1.7 in CARS. Never leave a question blank.
- When a section feels bad, reset; your feelings are not a reliable score predictor.
- Decide in advance the specific conditions under which you would void, and otherwise choose to score.

You're ready to start. Good luck.
