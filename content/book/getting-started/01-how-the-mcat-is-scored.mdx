Before you can plan your preparation, you need an accurate picture of what you are preparing for. The MCAT is long, and it is scored on an unusual scale, so a lot of anxious guesswork collects around it. Much of that anxiety goes away once you understand the structure: how many questions there are, how much time you get, how a raw count of correct answers becomes a number between 472 and 528, and what medical schools do with that number once they have it.

This chapter covers the stable facts about the exam. These are the parts that have not changed in years and are unlikely to change soon. Some details, such as registration fees, testing dates, the current percentile table and the exact retake limits, are updated by the Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC), which writes and administers the test. For those, we point you to the AAMC's own pages instead of quoting numbers that may be out of date by the time you read this.

## The Four Sections

The MCAT has four scored sections. They always come in the same order, and each one has its own clock.

| Order | Section | Questions | Time | What it tests |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Chemical and Physical Foundations of Biological Systems (C/P) | 59 | 95 min | General chemistry, organic chemistry, physics and biochemistry, set in biological contexts |
| 2 | Critical Analysis and Reasoning Skills (CARS) | 53 | 90 min | Reading comprehension and reasoning on humanities and social-science passages. No outside content is required. |
| 3 | Biological and Biochemical Foundations of Living Systems (B/B) | 59 | 95 min | Biology, biochemistry, and some organic and general chemistry |
| 4 | Psychological, Social, and Biological Foundations of Behavior (P/S) | 59 | 95 min | Introductory psychology and sociology, plus the biology of behavior |

That adds up to 230 questions and 375 minutes of testing, or 6 hours and 15 minutes. With the examinee agreement, the optional tutorial, the optional breaks and the short end-of-exam steps added on, the total seated time is about **7.5 hours**, before you count check-in. Plan for a full day, and train for one. The [Test Day](/getting-started/test-day) chapter walks through the day hour by hour.

<Callout type="high-yield" title="The numbers worth memorizing">

- Science sections (C/P, B/B, P/S): **59 questions in 95 minutes**, about 1.6 minutes per question.
- CARS: **53 questions in 90 minutes**, about 1.7 minutes per question.
- Each section is scored **118–132**, with a midpoint of **125**.
- The total is scored **472–528**, with a midpoint of **500**.
- There is **no penalty for guessing**, so never leave a question blank.

</Callout>

### Why the order matters

The fixed order affects how you should practice. C/P comes first, when you are fresh. P/S comes last, after more than six hours at the computer, when most people are at their most tired. Many students lose points in P/S not because they don't know the material but because they are reading carelessly by then. Full-length practice under realistic conditions is the only thing that trains for that, which is why the next chapter puts so much weight on it.

## Passage-Based and Discrete Questions

The MCAT has two question formats.

**Passage-based questions** come in sets attached to a passage. In the science sections, a passage is usually a few paragraphs describing an experiment, a clinical situation or a scientific concept, often with a figure, table or graph. Some questions in the set can be answered only from the passage. Others use the passage as a setting but test knowledge you bring with you.

**Discrete questions** stand alone. They have no passage, and they tend to test a single fact or concept more directly.

In each science section you can expect roughly **10 passages** carrying most of the questions, plus about **15 discrete questions** scattered between the passage sets. CARS is different: it is entirely passage-based, with about nine passages and no discretes.

### What this means for your preparation

Because most science questions sit inside a passage, the MCAT rewards two skills at once. You need content knowledge, and you need to pull information out of unfamiliar text and data quickly. A student who has memorized every amino acid but has never practiced reading a dense experimental passage under time pressure will underperform. So will a strong reader with gaps in content. Your preparation needs both, which is why this book pairs every chapter with passage-style practice.

<Callout type="trap" title="Treating the passage as optional">
Some students skip the passage and jump straight to the questions, assuming the questions only test outside knowledge. This works for a few questions per set at most. Many questions hinge on a specific detail in the passage, such as which mutant was used, what the control group received, or which axis a graph uses. Skimming strategically is fine. Skipping is not.
</Callout>

## From Raw Score to Scaled Score

Your **raw score** in a section is simply the number of questions you answered correctly. Nothing is subtracted for wrong answers. A wrong answer and a blank answer both earn zero, so a guess can only help you.

The raw score is then converted to a **scaled score** between 118 and 132. The four section scores are added together to give the total score, from 472 to 528.

The scale was designed so that the midpoint of each section (125) and of the total (500) sits near the middle of the distribution of test takers. Roughly speaking, a 500 is a middle-of-the-pack score and a 125 is a middle-of-the-pack section score. The actual percentile attached to any given score shifts a little from year to year.

### Equating: why the conversion varies by form

Many different versions, or *forms*, of the MCAT exist. They are built to the same blueprint, but no two sets of questions can be made exactly equal in difficulty. One form might happen to have a slightly harder physics passage; another might have an unusually gentle CARS passage.

To keep scores fair, the AAMC uses a statistical process called **equating**. Equating adjusts the raw-to-scaled conversion for each form so that a given scaled score means the same level of performance no matter which form you took. On a slightly harder form, a slightly lower raw score might earn the same scaled score as a slightly higher raw score on an easier form.

Three practical consequences follow:

1. **You can't know your exact conversion in advance.** Charts that claim "you need X correct for a 128" are approximations. They are useful for practice tests, but they don't apply exactly to your real form.
2. **You aren't graded against the people in your testing room.** The MCAT is not curved against the other students who tested on your day. Your score depends on how many questions you answered correctly and how difficult your form was, not on how well anyone else did.
3. **A "hard" exam isn't necessarily a bad sign.** If your exam felt brutal, that may simply mean your form was harder, and equating accounts for that. Don't spend a break convinced you have failed.

<Callout type="note" title="Unscored questions">
The AAMC includes some questions that are being tried out for future exams and do not count toward your score. They look exactly like scored questions, and you have no way of telling which ones they are. Treat every question as if it counts.
</Callout>

## Percentile Ranks

Along with your scaled scores, your score report shows **percentile ranks**: the percentage of recent test takers who scored at or below your score. A total score at the 80th percentile, for example, means you scored as high as or higher than about 80 percent of the comparison group.

Percentile ranks are recalculated periodically from recent testing data, so the percentile attached to a given score can drift a little over time. That's why we don't print a table here: any specific year's figures would eventually mislead someone. The AAMC publishes the current percentile ranks for each section and for the total score, and that is the table to use.

A few general patterns are stable:

- The midpoints (125 per section, 500 total) sit near the middle of the distribution.
- Because the distribution is bunched near the middle, a one-point change in a section score near 125 moves you through more percentile points than the same change near the top or bottom of the scale.
- Near the very top of the scale, each additional point is rarer and therefore harder to gain.

## Score Reports and Timing

Scores are not available on test day. They are typically released about a month after the exam, on a schedule the AAMC publishes in advance for each test date. Look up the release date for your specific test date when you register, and factor it into your application timeline. If you plan to apply early in an application cycle, a score that arrives a month after your exam needs to arrive in time.

Your score report includes:

- Your **four section scores** and your **total score**.
- **Percentile ranks** for each.
- **Confidence bands**, a short range around each score. These reflect the fact that any single test is an imperfect measure. If you took a different form on a different day, your score would probably fall somewhere in that band.
- A **score profile**, a visual summary showing your relative strengths and weaknesses across the four sections.

## How Medical Schools Use Your Scores

Admissions committees use the MCAT to answer a narrow question: is this applicant likely to handle the academic demands of medical school? The AAMC has published research showing that MCAT scores, together with undergraduate grades, help predict performance in the early years of medical school. The research does not show that the MCAT predicts who will be a good physician, and committees know that. So the MCAT is one factor among many, alongside your grades, experiences, letters, personal statement and interviews.

That said, the MCAT is often one of the first things a committee looks at, and some schools use it, together with grades, to decide which applications get a full read.

### The total and the score profile

Most schools look first at the total score. Many also look at the **score profile**, the balance among your four sections. Two applicants with a total of 512 can look quite different:

| | C/P | CARS | B/B | P/S | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Applicant A | 128 | 128 | 128 | 128 | 512 |
| Applicant B | 132 | 122 | 130 | 128 | 512 |

Many readers will see Applicant A as having no weak spot. Applicant B's 122 in CARS may raise a question, especially at schools that care a lot about reading and reasoning, or that have informal minimum section scores. Policies differ, and most schools don't publish rigid cutoffs, but a balanced profile is rarely a disadvantage. If one section is much lower than the others in your practice tests, it usually deserves a disproportionate share of your study time.

### Multiple attempts

If you take the MCAT more than once, schools generally see all of your scored attempts. Schools differ in how they weigh them: some focus on your most recent score, some on your highest, some consider the whole record. A steady upward trend generally reads well. Retaking without a real change in preparation, and scoring about the same, generally does not.

<Callout type="note" title="Score validity">
Most medical schools accept MCAT scores only from a limited window before the application cycle, commonly a few years. The window varies by school. If you are taking the MCAT well before you plan to apply, check that your score will still be accepted at the schools you're targeting when you apply.
</Callout>

## What Counts as a "Good" Score?

There is no single answer, and anyone who gives you one number is oversimplifying. A good score is one that makes you competitive for the schools and programs you are actually applying to. To work that out:

1. **Look at the published data.** The AAMC publishes summary statistics on applicants and matriculants, and its Medical School Admission Requirements (MSAR) resource lists MCAT ranges for individual schools. Historically, matriculant averages have sat comfortably above the 500 midpoint, but check the current figures rather than relying on a number you heard from a friend.
2. **Consider the whole application.** Strong grades and experiences can offset a score slightly below a school's median. A very high score rarely makes up for a thin application.
3. **Think in ranges, not thresholds.** A school's median is not its minimum. Many students are admitted below the median, and many above it are not.
4. **Consider program type.** Different programs, such as MD, DO, combined MD-PhD and others, have different typical ranges. So do schools with different missions.

When you set a target score, set it a little above where you need to be. Practice-test scores and real scores vary, and a buffer protects you from an ordinary bad day.

<Callout type="trap" title="Chasing a number in isolation">
Students sometimes pick a target score from an online forum, fall short of it in practice, and conclude that they must postpone indefinitely. Before you do that, ask what the score actually needs to accomplish for your list of schools. The answer is often less dramatic than the forum suggests. Equally often, the most useful move is to fix one weak section rather than to keep raising an already strong one.
</Callout>

## Retakes, Voids and Attempt Limits

You can take the MCAT more than once, but there are limits. The AAMC caps the number of attempts within a single testing year, across consecutive years, and over your lifetime. The exact limits are set and occasionally revised by the AAMC, so check its current policy before you register. Count every attempt carefully, because the caps apply whether or not you're happy with the result.

At the end of the exam you will be asked whether you want your exam **scored** or **voided**. A voided exam is not scored, and schools don't see a score for it. But it still counts as an attempt toward the limits. The [Test Day](/getting-started/test-day) chapter discusses how to make that decision calmly.

### Should you retake?

A retake makes sense when three things are true:

- Your score is meaningfully below what your school list requires, not just below a number you hoped for.
- You can identify specific, fixable reasons it was lower, such as a content gap, a pacing problem or a single weak section.
- You have the time and energy to change your preparation, not just repeat it.

If those conditions aren't met, a retake risks producing the same score, or a lower one, while using an attempt and delaying your application. Talk it through with a premedical advisor if you have access to one.

## Summary

- Four sections, always in the same order: C/P, CARS, B/B, P/S. The science sections have 59 questions in 95 minutes; CARS has 53 questions in 90 minutes. Expect about 7.5 hours of seated time.
- Science sections mix about 10 passages with about 15 discrete questions. CARS is all passages.
- Raw scores (the number correct, with no guessing penalty) are converted to scaled scores through equating, so the conversion varies by form. Sections run from 118 to 132 and totals from 472 to 528, with midpoints at 125 and 500.
- Percentile ranks, release dates, attempt limits and score-validity windows are set by the AAMC and individual schools. Check the current figures instead of relying on old ones.
- Schools look at the total and the balance among sections, in the context of the rest of your application.
