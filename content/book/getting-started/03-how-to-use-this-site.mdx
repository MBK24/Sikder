This site is built around a book. You read chapters, check your understanding as you go, and then put that understanding to work in practice questions, flashcards and timed tests. Every other feature exists to support that loop of learning, retrieving and reviewing.

This chapter is a tour. It explains what each part of the site does, where to find it and how it fits into the study plan from [Building a Study Schedule](/getting-started/building-a-study-schedule). At the end, we suggest a weekly workflow that uses the pieces together. You don't need to use every feature. Pick the ones that support your plan.

## The Book

The book is organized into parts. Part 0, Getting Started, is the part you're reading now. Then there is one part for each MCAT section: Chemical and Physical Foundations, CARS, Biological and Biochemical Foundations, and Psychological, Social, and Biological Foundations. An Appendix holds reference tables such as equations, amino acids, hormones and lab techniques.

### Finding your way around

- **The left sidebar** lists every part and chapter. Chapters that are planned but not yet written appear greyed out, so you can see what's coming.
- **The right sidebar, "On this page,"** is a table of contents for the chapter you're reading. Select any entry to jump straight to that section.
- **Section numbers** appear automatically. In the section parts, a chapter's sections are numbered, so the second section of the second chemistry chapter is CP 2.2 and its first subsection is CP 2.2.1. These numbers make it easy to refer to a precise spot, for example in a forum post or your error log. The Getting Started and Appendix chapters are unnumbered.
- **Prev and Next links** at the bottom of each chapter take you through the book in order.

### Reading tools

- **Light and dark modes.** Use the toggle to switch. Dark mode is easier on the eyes for long evening sessions.
- **The reading-progress indicator** shows how far through the current chapter you are.
- **"Mark as complete."** Each chapter has a button at the end. Marking a chapter complete records your progress, so you can see how much of your content review is done. Mark a chapter complete when you have read it *and* done its questions, not just when you've scrolled to the bottom.

## Callout Boxes

Throughout the book, short boxes set apart information that deserves special attention. There are four main kinds, each with its own purpose. Here is a live example of each.

<Callout type="high-yield">
Facts the exam tests again and again. We use these sparingly, about one per section at most, so that they keep their meaning. If you're short on time, reading just the High-Yield boxes in a chapter is a reasonable last-minute review. For example: the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation, $\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a + \log\frac{[\text{A}^-]}{[\text{HA}]}$, tells you that pH equals pKa when the acid and its conjugate base are present in equal amounts.
</Callout>

<Callout type="trap">
A specific wrong answer that students tend to choose, and why it's wrong. For example: many students assume a catalyst shifts an equilibrium toward products. It doesn't. A catalyst speeds up the forward and reverse reactions equally, so equilibrium is reached faster, but the equilibrium constant and position are unchanged. When you see a Common Trap box, read it slowly. It describes a mistake you are likely to make.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mnemonic">
A memory device for a list or a sequence. For example: the order of the mitotic phases is **P**rophase, **P**rometaphase, **M**etaphase, **A**naphase, **T**elophase, which some students remember as "**P**lease **P**ass **M**e **A**nother **T**owel." Mnemonics are tools for storage, not understanding, so pair each one with the concept behind it.
</Callout>

<Callout type="connection" to="/bb/amino-acids-and-proteins">
A link between topics in different sections, because the MCAT loves to cross boundaries. This box, for example, points to the chapter on amino acids and proteins. The acid–base chemistry you learn in Chemical and Physical Foundations is exactly what determines an amino acid's charge at a given pH. Connection boxes are clickable: select one to jump to the linked chapter.
</Callout>

You'll also see occasional **Note** boxes for anything else worth setting apart, such as a clarification, an exception or a piece of context.

## Inline Questions and Chapter Quizzes

### Inline practice questions

Most chapters include a few practice questions placed right after the sections they test. They are MCAT-style multiple-choice questions with four answer choices. When you select an answer, the site shows whether you were right, explains the correct answer and, for many questions, explains why each wrong choice is wrong.

Your answers are recorded. That means your analytics can track your accuracy on each topic, and the question bank knows which questions you have already seen. Answer honestly, without scrolling back up first: the point is retrieval practice, not a perfect record.

### End-of-chapter quizzes

Each chapter in the four section parts ends with a quiz of about 10–15 questions. Quizzes mix straightforward recall with application, and several questions are set inside a short experiment or clinical scenario, the way real MCAT discrete questions are.

When you finish a quiz, you get a **per-topic breakdown** of your results, organized by AAMC content category. This tells you which parts of the chapter you've got and which ones to revisit before moving on.

## The Question Bank

The question bank holds all of the site's practice questions in one place, including questions that appear only there. Use it once you've covered some content and want targeted or timed practice.

### Filtering

You can filter questions by:

| Filter | Options | Use it to |
|---|---|---|
| Section | C/P, CARS, B/B, P/S | Focus on one section at a time |
| Topic | AAMC content categories | Drill a weak area identified by your analytics or error log |
| Difficulty | Intro, core, advanced | Warm up on easier questions or push yourself on harder ones |
| Status | Unseen, correct, incorrect, flagged | Do new questions, retry ones you missed, or return to ones you flagged |

**Flagging** is useful during practice. If you get a question right but aren't sure why, flag it and come back later.

### Timed practice sets and section tests

- **Timed practice sets** let you choose a number of questions and work through them against a clock. They're good for building pacing in short sessions.
- **Full-length section tests** reproduce a complete MCAT section with real MCAT timing: 59 questions in 95 minutes for each science section, and 53 questions in 90 minutes for CARS. Use them in the practice phase to rehearse a full section under realistic pressure.

## Flashcards

The flashcard system uses spaced repetition, the technique described in the previous chapter. After you see a card, you tell the system how well you remembered it. Cards you know well come back at increasingly long intervals. Cards you miss come back soon. Over time, you spend most of your review on what you're about to forget, instead of on what you already know.

There are two kinds of decks:

- **Built-in decks**, one for each chapter, written to go with the chapter's content. Add a chapter's deck when you start reading that chapter.
- **Your own decks.** Write cards for things you personally keep forgetting, especially items from your error log. Cards you write yourself, phrased as questions, are some of the most effective study tools you can make.

Keep your daily review short and consistent. Fifteen to thirty minutes every day is far more effective than two hours once a week.

## Analytics

The analytics page turns your activity into a picture of where you stand. It shows:

- **Accuracy by topic**, across inline questions, quizzes and the question bank, organized by AAMC content category.
- **Time per question**, so you can spot topics where you're slow even when you're accurate.
- **Your weakest areas**, ranked, to help you decide what to study next.
- **Your study streak**, the number of consecutive days you've studied.
- **Estimated readiness**, a rough indicator based on your coverage and performance so far.

Treat estimated readiness as a guide, not a prediction of your real score. Official practice exams taken under realistic conditions remain the best evidence of where you stand. Use analytics mainly to make decisions, for example "my accuracy on 4C is low and I'm slow there, so electrochemistry goes on next week's plan."

## The Q&A Forum

The forum is where you can ask questions about anything that isn't clicking, and read answers to questions other students have already asked.

- **Categories** are organized by MCAT section, so a question about buffers goes in a different place from a question about CARS strategy.
- **"Discuss this chapter" links** appear in each chapter and take you straight to the forum discussions about that chapter. Because sections have stable numbers and anchors, you can point to the exact passage you're asking about.
- **Verified tutors and experts** have badges next to their names, and their answers are highlighted, so you can quickly find a reliable explanation.
- **Upvotes** help the most useful answers rise to the top.
- **Accepted answers** are marked, so you can see which answer resolved the question.

Before posting, search to see whether someone has already asked your question. When you post, say what you have already tried and where your reasoning breaks down. That gets you better answers, and writing it out often solves the problem on its own.

## Putting It All Together

Here is one way to combine these tools across the phases described in the previous chapter.

### During content review

For each chapter:

1. **Read actively.** Use the "On this page" table of contents to preview the chapter's structure first. Read one section at a time.
2. **Answer the inline questions** as you reach them, before you look back at the text.
3. **Take the end-of-chapter quiz** and look at the per-topic breakdown. Reread any section where you missed more than one question.
4. **Add the chapter's flashcard deck**, plus a few cards of your own for anything that surprised you.
5. **Mark the chapter complete.**
6. **Use "Discuss this chapter"** if anything is still unclear.

Every day, do your flashcard review.

### During practice

1. **Check analytics weekly** to identify your weakest topics.
2. **Use the question bank filters** to drill those topics, starting with unseen questions, then incorrect ones.
3. **Take timed practice sets** a few times a week, and a **full-length section test** for your weakest section regularly.
4. **Add missed questions to your error log**, turn the facts behind them into flashcards, and reread the relevant book sections.

### During final review

1. **Retake flagged and incorrect questions** in the question bank.
2. **Skim the High-Yield and Common Trap boxes** across the book for a quick sweep of the most-tested points.
3. **Keep the Appendix open** while you review, for equations, amino acids, hormones and lab techniques.
4. **Keep up your daily flashcards**, right up to the last few days before the exam.

## Summary

- The book is the core: read with the sidebars and section numbers, and mark chapters complete when you have done their questions.
- Callout boxes flag high-yield facts, common traps, mnemonics and connections across sections.
- Inline questions and chapter quizzes record your answers and feed your analytics and question-bank status.
- The question bank, flashcards and analytics work together: analytics shows what's weak, the bank drills it, and flashcards keep it from fading.
- The forum is there when you're stuck, with verified tutors and experts highlighted.
