The previous chapter, on [reading for structure](/cars/reading-for-structure), was about the passage: how to see an argument's shape and record it in a map. This chapter is about the questions. CARS uses a small number of recurring question types, and each one asks you to consult a particular part of your map in a particular way. Once you can recognize a type from its stem, you know where to look and what kind of answer to expect before you read a single choice.

The second half of the chapter turns to the choices themselves. Wrong answers on CARS are not random. They are built from a handful of predictable distortions of the passage, and learning to name those distortions turns elimination from a matter of gut feeling into a repeatable procedure. Everything here is illustrated with two original passages, printed below, so you can watch each method work on real text.

## How CARS Questions Work

Every CARS question has the same underlying logic. The stem asks about some relationship in the passage. The correct answer is a **paraphrase** of something the passage says or clearly implies, sometimes extended to a new case. The three wrong answers are **distortions**: statements that sound related to the passage but change its meaning in a specific way.

That logic suggests a routine you should apply to every question:

1. **Read the stem and classify it.** What type is it? Does it point to a specific paragraph or phrase?
2. **Go back to the passage or your map.** For a main-idea question, consult your one-sentence summary. For a detail, function or definition question, reread the referenced sentence plus one sentence on either side.
3. **Predict.** Form a rough answer in your own words before looking at the choices.
4. **Eliminate.** Match each choice against your prediction and against the passage, crossing out choices that contain a recognizable distortion.

<Callout type="high-yield" title="The passage is the only authority">
The correct answer to every CARS question is supported by the passage. If you find yourself defending a choice with something you know from a class or from the news, stop: you are probably looking at an out-of-scope distractor. Even "Reasoning Beyond the Text" questions extend the *author's* logic; they never ask whether the author is right.
</Callout>

## Two Practice Passages

Read both passages now and build a map for each. Every worked example in the chapter refers to them as Passage A and Passage B.

<Callout type="note" title="Passage A: Cleaning the Past">

**¶1** When a museum announces that a centuries-old painting has been cleaned, the reaction is often divided. Some viewers are delighted by colors that had been hidden for generations beneath yellowed varnish and grime; others feel that something has been stripped away. The dispute is usually framed as a contest between two camps: those who would return a work to the state its maker intended, and those who would preserve the marks that time has left on it. Each camp can point to cleanings that seem to vindicate it, and each has its celebrated disasters.

**¶2** The first camp has the stronger intuitive appeal. A varnish that has darkened to the color of weak tea was no part of the painter's design, and to leave it in place is to let an accident of chemistry overrule a deliberate artistic choice. Yet "the artist's intention" is a more slippery standard than it appears. Painters frequently applied thin tinted glazes over their colors to soften or unify them, and these glazes sit so close to the later varnish that a restorer cannot always tell, even under magnification, where one ends and the other begins. An aggressive cleaning, undertaken in the name of the original, may remove part of it.

**¶3** The second camp answers that the patina of age is itself part of a work's meaning. A painting that has passed through four centuries is not the object that left the studio, and pretending otherwise falsifies its history. This argument has real force, but it proves too much. If every alteration time produces is meaningful, then so are the flaking, the fading of unstable pigments and the overpainting by later hands, none of which even the most devoted advocate of patina would wish to keep in every case.

**¶4** What both positions share is the hope that a single principle could settle every case in advance. Restoration rarely permits such comfort. Paintings differ in their materials, their histories and the damage they have suffered, and a rule that suits one may ruin another. The more defensible practice, I think, is one of disciplined restraint: removing what can be confidently identified as later accretion, leaving what cannot, and documenting each decision so that a future restorer, with better instruments, can revisit it. Such a practice will satisfy neither camp entirely. A restrained cleaning leaves some paintings looking neither new nor old, and visitors may find the result unsatisfying.

**¶5** But the dissatisfaction is instructive. It reminds us that a restored painting is always a negotiation between the past and the present, and that the restorer's task is not to recover a lost original but to avoid making errors that cannot be undone. Reversibility, more than authenticity, may be the virtue the field most needs.

</Callout>

<Callout type="note" title="Passage B: Whose Categories?">

**¶1** Anthropologists have long distinguished between describing a culture in its own terms and describing it in the analyst's. The first approach, often called emic, tries to reconstruct the distinctions that matter to the people being studied: how they themselves sort kin from strangers, the sacred from the ordinary, work from play. The second, called etic, applies categories devised by the observer, such as "household", "economy" or "religion", that are meant to permit comparison across societies. The terms derive from linguistics, where a parallel distinction separates the sounds that speakers of a language treat as different from the sounds an instrument can measure.

**¶2** For much of the last century, the etic approach carried the prestige of science. If anthropology was to say anything general about human beings, it seemed, it needed a vocabulary that did not change every time the fieldworker moved to a new valley. Categories such as "kinship" and "economy" allowed researchers to set findings from distant continents side by side and to ask what, if anything, all societies share. Critics have since argued that this vocabulary was never as neutral as it claimed. "Religion", for example, took shape in European debates about churches and creeds; applied elsewhere, it can lead an observer to look for doctrine where people are chiefly concerned with practice, and to conclude that a community is "less religious" simply because its devotion does not take a familiar form.

**¶3** The criticism is just, but its most enthusiastic proponents draw from it a conclusion it does not support. To say that outside categories distort is not to show that inside categories are transparent. People's own accounts of their lives are shaped by interest, by what they assume a visitor wants to hear, and by matters so habitual that they never think to mention them. A farmer may describe the division of labor in his village as a matter of custom while the village's land records suggest that it tracks wealth quite closely.

**¶4** The more promising course is to treat the two vocabularies as checks on each other. An etic category is a hypothesis: it proposes that something called "household" in one place is comparable to something called by that name elsewhere. Emic description tests the hypothesis by asking whether the people concerned draw the line where the category assumes. When they do not, the category must be revised, though not abandoned, since without some shared terms comparison becomes impossible and anthropology dissolves into a collection of unrelated portraits.

**¶5** This view asks more of the fieldworker than either alternative does, and it offers less certainty. But anthropology has never been a discipline that rewards certainty, and its best work has typically come from those willing to hold two ways of seeing in tension rather than resolve them prematurely.

</Callout>

Quick maps. **A:** (1) two camps; (2) intention appealing but slippery (glazes); (3) patina has force but proves too much; (4) author: disciplined, documented restraint; (5) aim at reversibility. **B:** (1) emic vs etic defined; (2) critics: etic not neutral (religion); (3) author agrees, but insiders aren't transparent (farmer); (4) author: mutual checks, revise not abandon; (5) harder but best.

## Comprehension Questions

Foundations of Comprehension questions ask what the passage says: its main point, a specific claim, the meaning of a term, or the author's tone. They are the most direct questions in the section, and they reward a good map more than any other type.

### Main Idea and Primary Purpose

**How it is phrased:** "The main idea of the passage is that...", "The central thesis is...", "The primary purpose of the passage is to..." A primary-purpose question asks for the same thing as a main-idea question but describes it with a verb: *argue*, *explain*, *challenge*, *describe*, *propose*.

**How to attack it:** Consult your one-sentence summary. For purpose questions, also check the verb. An author who takes a position is *arguing*, not *describing*; an author who surveys a debate without siding is *describing*, not *defending*.

**Worked example (Passage A).** *The primary purpose of the passage is to:*

- A. trace the history of disputes over cleaning old paintings
- B. defend the view that a painting's patina is part of its meaning
- C. argue that neither camp suffices and recommend cautious, reversible practice
- D. argue that museums should stop cleaning old paintings altogether

*Prediction:* to argue that no single principle works and that restoration should aim at restraint and reversibility. **C** matches. A has the wrong verb and scope: the passage traces no history. B is half of the debate, and the author says that view "proves too much." D is extreme; the author recommends removing what can be confidently identified, which is still cleaning.

### Detail and Retrieval

**How it is phrased:** "According to the passage...", "The passage states that...", "The author claims that..."

**How to attack it:** These are the only questions where the answer is stated almost outright. Find the sentence, reread it with its neighbors, and choose the closest paraphrase. The risk is not difficulty but carelessness: distractors often borrow words from the right sentence while changing its claim.

**Worked example (Passage B).** *According to the passage, critics object that the category "religion":*

- A. was invented by anthropologists to make cultures easier to compare
- B. took shape in European debates and can lead observers to look for doctrine
- C. cannot be translated into the languages of the communities being studied
- D. has been abandoned by most anthropologists since the last century

The answer is in paragraph 2: "religion" "took shape in European debates about churches and creeds" and can lead an observer "to look for doctrine." **B** paraphrases this. A distorts the origin (European debates, not anthropologists' invention). C and D are out of scope: the passage says nothing about translation or about the category's being abandoned.

### Definition in Context

**How it is phrased:** "As used in paragraph 4, the word 'hypothesis' most nearly means...", "The author's use of the phrase X suggests..."

**How to attack it:** Ignore the dictionary. CARS chooses words that the author uses in a specialized or extended sense, and the most common wrong answer is the word's ordinary meaning. Reread the sentence and predict a replacement word or phrase that would fit the author's meaning there.

**Worked example (Passage B).** *As used in paragraph 4, "hypothesis" most nearly means:*

- A. a scientific prediction confirmed by controlled experiment
- B. a speculative guess with no evidence behind it
- C. a provisional claim of comparability that is open to testing
- D. a conclusion reached through extended field observation

The author explains the term in the next clause: an etic category "proposes that something called 'household' in one place is comparable to something called by that name elsewhere", and emic description "tests" it. **C** captures both halves: a proposal of comparability, and openness to testing. A is the textbook meaning and adds confirmation the passage denies. B is too dismissive; nothing suggests the categories lack basis. D is the opposite: a hypothesis is tested, not concluded.

### Tone and Attitude

**How it is phrased:** "The author's attitude toward X is best described as...", "The author would most likely characterize X as...", "The tone of the passage is..."

**How to attack it:** Find the evaluative words about X and any concessions. Then choose the answer that matches both the *direction* (positive, negative, mixed) and the *intensity* of the language.

**Worked example (Passage B).** *The author's attitude toward the critics of the etic approach is best described as:*

- A. agreement with their criticism but not with the conclusion some draw from it
- B. complete agreement, since they exposed the false neutrality of etic categories
- C. dismissive impatience with their attack on scientific comparison
- D. strict neutrality, since the author reports their view without evaluating it

"The criticism is just, but its most enthusiastic proponents draw from it a conclusion it does not support." That one sentence holds both halves of **A**. B is half-right (the author does agree with the criticism) but ignores the "but." C is the opposite of "just." D contradicts the evaluative word "just."

<Callout type="trap" title="The dictionary definition">
On definition-in-context questions, at least one choice is almost always the word's most common meaning. It feels safe because it is true *in general*. It is wrong because CARS asks only about the meaning in this sentence, and the author has usually signaled a narrower or extended sense nearby.
</Callout>

## Reasoning Within the Text

Reasoning Within the Text questions ask how the parts of the passage relate. The answer is not stated in any single sentence; it is found in the connection between sentences, which is exactly what your map records.

### Inference

**How it is phrased:** "The author would most likely agree that...", "The passage suggests/implies that...", "It can be inferred that..."

**How to attack it:** An inference on CARS is a *small* step from the text, not a creative leap. The correct answer is something the author did not quite say but would have to accept given what they did say. Test each choice by asking, "Would the author object to this?" If the author would plausibly object, eliminate it.

**Worked example (Passage A).** *The author would most likely agree that, when a restorer cannot tell whether a layer is original:*

- A. the layer should be removed, since varnish usually darkens with age
- B. the layer should be left in place and the decision recorded
- C. the painting should be withdrawn from display until the question is settled
- D. the restorer should rely on what visitors find most satisfying

Paragraph 4 recommends "removing what can be confidently identified as later accretion, leaving what cannot, and documenting each decision." **B** applies that directly. A reverses the rule. C is out of scope. D contradicts the passage, which accepts that visitors "may find the result unsatisfying."

### Function

**How it is phrased:** "The author mentions X in order to...", "The reference to X serves primarily to...", "Which of the following best describes the role of paragraph 3?"

**How to attack it:** Ask what claim the detail supports, and in which paragraph. Your map's purpose labels do most of the work. A common wrong answer accurately describes *what* the detail says but not *why* it is there.

**Worked example (Passage A).** *The author mentions "the flaking, the fading of unstable pigments and the overpainting by later hands" (paragraph 3) primarily in order to:*

- A. show that the patina argument, taken to its limit, would protect damage its advocates would remove
- B. list the forms of deterioration that restorers most often treat
- C. support the first camp's view that varnish is an accident of chemistry
- D. explain why each restoration decision must be documented for the future

The list follows "it proves too much" and is introduced by "If every alteration time produces is meaningful, then so are..." It is the evidence for the claim that the patina argument proves too much. **A** states this. B describes the content, not the purpose. C ties the detail to the wrong paragraph's argument. D connects it to paragraph 4, where it does not appear.

<Callout type="note" title="What versus why">
Inference and function questions share a trap: a choice that is *accurate about the passage* but answers a different question. On a function question, any choice that merely restates the detail ("lists forms of deterioration") answers "what" when the stem asked "why." Always link the detail to the claim it serves.
</Callout>

## Reasoning Beyond the Text

These questions make up about 40% of the section. Each one introduces something the passage does not contain (a new finding, a new case, a parallel situation) and asks you to connect it to the author's reasoning. They feel open-ended, but the logic is the same as everywhere else: the answer follows from the author's argument, not from your opinion.

### Strengthen and Weaken

**How it is phrased:** "Which of the following findings, if true, would most strengthen/weaken the author's claim that...?", "Which would most challenge the critics' argument?", "The author's argument depends on which assumption?"

**How to attack it:** First identify the precise claim being tested and the reason the author gives for it. Then look for the choice that bears directly on that reason. A strong weakener attacks the premise the claim depends on; a strong strengthener supplies evidence the claim needs. Choices that affect a *different* claim in the passage, however dramatic, are irrelevant.

**Worked example (Passage B).** *Which finding would most weaken the author's claim that people's own accounts of their lives are not transparent?*

- A. Across many villages, residents' descriptions of how labor is divided closely match land records and observed work.
- B. Fieldworkers using etic categories frequently misclassify devotional practices as non-religious.
- C. Residents describe their customs differently to outside visitors than to one another.
- D. Comparative studies using the category "household" have produced influential findings.

The claim rests on the idea that insiders' accounts can conceal factors such as wealth, illustrated by the farmer whose description diverges from the land records. **A** shows the opposite pattern on a large scale, undermining the claim's support. B bears on the critics' point about etic categories, not on insiders' transparency. C *strengthens* the claim (accounts are shaped by "what they assume a visitor wants to hear"). D is true-but-irrelevant to this claim.

### New Information and Application

**How it is phrased:** "Suppose that... Based on the passage, the author would most likely...", "Which of the following situations is most consistent with the author's recommendation?", "How would the author respond to...?"

**How to attack it:** Restate the author's principle in general terms, then apply it to the new case as the author would. Pay attention to the author's qualifications: if the author recommends removal only when identification is *confident*, a new tool that makes identification confident changes the answer.

**Worked example (Passage A).** *Suppose a new imaging technique allows restorers to distinguish a painter's glazes from later varnish reliably. The author would most likely:*

- A. welcome its use to remove varnish that could not previously be identified with confidence
- B. oppose its use, because the marks of age are part of a painting's meaning
- C. conclude that the dispute between the two camps has now been settled
- D. argue that documenting restoration decisions is no longer necessary

The author's rule removes "what can be confidently identified as later accretion" and expects "a future restorer, with better instruments" to revisit past decisions. A reliable technique enlarges what can be confidently identified, so **A** follows. B is the patina camp's voice. C is extreme: the technique would resolve one difficulty, not the author's claim that no single principle settles every case. D is the opposite of the author's emphasis on reversibility.

### Analogy

**How it is phrased:** "Which of the following is most analogous to...?", "The author's view of X is most similar to which of the following?"

**How to attack it:** Strip the passage's relationship down to an abstract structure, then find the choice with the same structure in a different setting. Ignore surface similarity in subject matter; test writers often make a wrong choice resemble the passage's *topic* while breaking its *logic*.

**Worked example (Passage B).** *The relationship the author proposes between emic and etic description is most similar to that of:*

- A. a translator who leaves untranslatable words in the original language
- B. a mapmaker who uses a standard grid but redraws borders after consulting local residents
- C. a traveler who relies entirely on a local guide to interpret a foreign city
- D. a census taker who applies one national form to every household without revision

Abstract structure: a shared comparative framework (etic) that is kept but revised when insiders draw the lines differently (emic). **B** has both elements. A abandons the shared vocabulary for some terms. C is purely emic. D is purely etic, with no revision.

<Callout type="high-yield" title="Strengthen and weaken: aim at the reason">
The best strengthener or weakener almost always targets the *evidence or premise* behind the claim, not the claim's conclusion. Before reading the choices, ask: "Why does the author believe this?" Then look for the choice that makes that reason more or less believable. A choice that leaves the reason untouched cannot be the strongest answer.
</Callout>

## Predict Before You Read the Choices

The single most effective habit in CARS question work is forming an answer in your own words *before* looking at the choices. Prediction matters because CARS distractors are designed to be persuasive when read in isolation. Each wrong answer contains real phrases from the passage and sounds like something a thoughtful reader might say. If you read the choices with no expectation, you will judge each one by whether it sounds reasonable, and three or four of them will.

A prediction changes the task. Instead of evaluating four statements on their own merits, you compare each to a target. The comparison is faster and far more reliable.

### What a Good Prediction Looks Like

Predictions do not need to be precise. For a main-idea question, your one-sentence summary is the prediction. For a function question, a few words naming the claim the detail supports ("evidence that patina argument proves too much") is enough. For a strengthen or weaken question, the prediction is a *description* of what the right answer must do ("something showing insiders' accounts are accurate"), since you cannot predict the specific finding.

### When You Cannot Predict

Some stems are too open to predict, such as "Which of the following is supported by the passage?" with no further guidance. For these, skip to elimination but raise the bar: each choice must be traceable to a specific sentence. If you cannot point to the line that supports a choice, it is not supported.

<Callout type="trap" title="Falling for the first plausible choice">
Students who skip prediction often choose the first choice that sounds right, without reading the rest carefully. On CARS, choice A or B is frequently a polished distractor that is close to correct. Always read all four choices, and always compare them to your prediction rather than to each other.
</Callout>

## A Systematic Elimination Method

Even with a prediction, you will often face two choices that both seem plausible. Elimination resolves these cases by giving you a vocabulary for what is wrong with a choice. Wrong CARS answers fall into six recurring types, and naming the type is usually enough to cross a choice out with confidence.

| Trap type | What it does | Signal |
|---|---|---|
| **Scope: too broad or too narrow** | States a claim larger than the passage argues, or captures only one part of it | Answer extends to "all art" or "anthropology as a whole"; or describes only one paragraph |
| **Extreme language** | Strengthens the author's qualified claim | always, never, must, entirely, proven, only, all; the passage said "often", "may", "I think" |
| **Out of scope / outside knowledge** | Introduces something the passage never discusses, even if true in the real world | You can't find the topic anywhere in the passage |
| **Opposite** | Reverses the author's claim, or states the view the author rejects | The answer matches the critics, or the passage says the reverse |
| **Half-right, half-wrong** | Begins accurately, then adds an unsupported or contradicted element | One clause is correct; the rest, often after "because" or "since", is not |
| **True but irrelevant** | Accurately reflects the passage but does not answer the question asked | It is supported, but it describes "what" when the stem asks "why", or concerns the wrong claim |

### Applying the Method

Work through the choices one at a time and, for each, ask whether it contains one of the six distortions. Cross out a choice the moment you find one. When you finish, you will usually have one survivor. If you have two, compare them word by word: the difference between them is almost always a single word or clause, and that clause is where one of them breaks.

Two refinements make the method more reliable:

- **Half-right choices fail at the joint.** Read every word of the second half of a choice, especially after "because", "since" or "and therefore." Test writers put the error there knowing that students stop reading once the first half checks out.
- **Wrong voice is the most common opposite.** A choice that accurately states the critics' view is "opposite" if the question asks for the author's. Before eliminating, check your map's voice tags.

<Callout type="mnemonic" title="Wrong answers aren't HONEST">
**H**alf-right, **O**pposite, **N**ot in the passage (out of scope or outside knowledge), **E**xtreme, **S**cope too broad or narrow, **T**rue but irrelevant. Each wrong choice is dishonest about the passage in one of these six ways. Name the trap aloud during practice review; on test day the label will come automatically.
</Callout>

## EXCEPT, NOT and LEAST Questions

Some stems reverse the usual task: "The author makes each of the following claims EXCEPT...", "Which of the following is NOT supported by the passage?", "Which would LEAST weaken the argument?" These questions take longer than they should because the mind keeps slipping back into the normal mode of looking for the true answer.

### The True/False Method

Convert the question into a sorting task. For each choice, mark **T** if the passage supports it and **F** if it does not. Three choices will be T; the one F is your answer. For LEAST questions, rank the choices by how much they affect the claim and pick the weakest effect.

Write the T or F next to each letter on your noteboard. The physical act of labeling breaks the habit of looking for the "right-sounding" answer, which on an EXCEPT question is precisely what you must *not* choose.

**Worked example (Passage B).** *The author suggests each of the following as a reason that people's accounts of their own lives may be misleading EXCEPT:*

- A. their own interests in how things are described
- B. their assumptions about what a visitor wants to hear
- C. habits so familiar they never think to mention them
- D. their lack of a vocabulary that permits comparison

Paragraph 3 lists interest (A: T), "what they assume a visitor wants to hear" (B: T) and "matters so habitual that they never think to mention them" (C: T). D sounds plausible because the passage discusses comparative vocabulary, but that point concerns etic categories in paragraphs 2 and 4, not insiders' reliability. D is the only F, so **D** is correct.

### Pitfalls Specific to Reversed Questions

The out-of-scope trap is the usual right answer to an EXCEPT question: three choices come from the passage and one does not. But beware of a choice that comes from the passage *in a different context*, as D did above. It is in the passage, but not as an answer to the stem's question, so it is still the exception.

<Callout type="trap" title="Forgetting the reversal halfway through">
The most common error on EXCEPT questions is evaluating the first two choices correctly, then reverting to normal mode and picking the first choice that "sounds right." Writing T or F beside every letter, and choosing the lone F, prevents this.
</Callout>

## When to Flag and Move On

CARS gives you about 10 minutes per passage, and every question is worth the same. A question that consumes three minutes costs you more than the question itself; it steals time from easier questions in later passages. Knowing when to stop is part of the skill.

### A Practical Rule

If you have predicted, eliminated, and are still split between two choices after about 90 seconds on a question, **choose the better of the two, flag it, and move on.** Your best guess between two finalists is right more often than not, and if time remains at the end you can return with fresh eyes. Never leave a question blank; there is no penalty for wrong answers.

### What Usually Deserves a Flag

- Questions that require you to hold several pieces of new information at once, such as a complex hypothetical scenario
- EXCEPT questions where two choices both seem unsupported
- Questions on a paragraph you mapped poorly and would need to reread in full

### Returning to Flagged Questions

When you return, do not reread the whole passage. Reread the stem, consult your map, and look again only at the two finalists. Often the time away is enough: a word you skimmed the first time ("only", "because", "most") will stand out and settle the choice.

<Callout type="note" title="Practice your exits">
During timed practice, record how long you spend on each question and how often your flagged answers were right. Most students discover two things: they spend far too long on a few questions, and their quick "best guess" on those questions was correct more often than they expected. That data makes it easier to let go on test day.
</Callout>

Know what each question asks, predict before reading the choices, and eliminate by naming specific distortions rather than by feel. Combined with the passage map from the previous chapter, that is a complete method for every question the section can ask.
