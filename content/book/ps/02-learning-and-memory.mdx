**Learning** is a relatively lasting change in behavior or knowledge that results from experience. The definition excludes changes caused by maturation, fatigue or drugs, and it covers far more than classroom study. A sea slug that stops withdrawing its gill after harmless touches has learned. So has a dog that drools at the sound of a can opener and a child who copies her brother's swearing.

**Memory** is the capacity to encode, store and retrieve information over time, and it is what makes learning possible. The MCAT tests this chapter in two ways. The vocabulary is precise and the distractors exploit imprecision: negative reinforcement versus punishment, proactive versus retroactive interference, extinction versus forgetting. And nearly every concept has a landmark experiment that the exam likes to describe in new clothes. This chapter gives you the definitions, the studies, and the neuroscience that links them to B/B.

## Habituation, Sensitization, and Dishabituation

The simplest forms of learning are **non-associative**: they change the response to a single stimulus, with no pairing of events.

**Habituation** is a decrease in response to a repeated stimulus that turns out to be harmless. The first few trains past a new apartment wake you; after a few weeks you sleep through them. Habituation frees attention for stimuli that might matter.

**Sensitization** is an increase in response to a stimulus, usually after exposure to a strong or noxious one. After hearing a gunshot, people startle more to an ordinary door slam. Sensitization is less stimulus-specific than habituation; one alarming event heightens responses to many stimuli.

**Dishabituation** is the recovery of a habituated response after a new or strong stimulus is presented. If you have tuned out a refrigerator's hum and a loud crash sounds in the kitchen, the hum may suddenly become noticeable again. Dishabituation shows that habituation is learning, not fatigue: a tired neuron or muscle would not recover just because a different stimulus appeared.

Researchers exploit habituation to study infants. A baby is shown the same picture until her looking time falls; then a new picture appears. If looking time jumps back up, she must have detected a difference.

### The cellular basis of simple learning

Eric Kandel's Nobel Prize-winning work on the sea slug *Aplysia* revealed the cellular mechanisms. Touching the siphon triggers a protective gill withdrawal. With repeated gentle touches the reflex habituates because the sensory neuron releases less neurotransmitter (glutamate) onto the motor neuron. A shock to the tail produces sensitization: interneurons release serotonin onto the sensory neuron's terminals, increasing its transmitter release through a cAMP pathway. With enough repetition, these short-term changes become long-term ones that require new protein synthesis and new synapses. Learning, in other words, is a physical change in synapses.

<Callout type="trap" title="Habituation is not sensory adaptation">
Sensory adaptation is a decrease in *receptor* responsiveness to a constant stimulus, and a novel stimulus does not undo it. Habituation is a learned decrease in a *behavioral response*, produced in the central nervous system, and dishabituation reverses it instantly. If a response disappears and then returns after an unrelated loud noise, the question is describing habituation.
</Callout>

## Classical Conditioning

**Associative learning** means learning that events are connected. In classical conditioning, an organism learns that two *stimuli* go together. In operant conditioning, it learns that a *behavior* leads to a consequence.

### Pavlov and the vocabulary of conditioning

Ivan Pavlov, a physiologist studying digestion, noticed that his dogs began salivating before food reached them, at the sight of the assistant who fed them. In his experiments a neutral sound, such as a metronome or tone, was presented just before food. After several pairings, the sound alone produced salivation.

| Term | Definition | In Pavlov's experiment |
|---|---|---|
| Unconditioned stimulus (UCS) | Naturally and automatically triggers a response | Food in the mouth |
| Unconditioned response (UCR) | The unlearned response to the UCS | Salivation to food |
| Neutral stimulus (NS) | Initially produces no relevant response | The tone, before training |
| Conditioned stimulus (CS) | A former NS that triggers a response after pairing with the UCS | The tone, after training |
| Conditioned response (CR) | The learned response to the CS | Salivation to the tone |

The UCR and CR are often the same behavior; what distinguishes them is the stimulus that triggers them. Classical conditioning governs involuntary responses such as salivation, fear, nausea and heart rate. It underlies many phobias, the anticipatory nausea some chemotherapy patients feel on entering the clinic, and advertising that pairs products with attractive images.

<Callout type="note" title="Labeling a conditioning scenario">
Work backward from the response. Find the response that occurs without any learning: that is the UCR, and whatever caused it is the UCS. Then find the stimulus that produces the response only because of its pairing with the UCS: that is the CS, and the learned response to it is the CR.
</Callout>

### Acquisition, extinction and spontaneous recovery

**Acquisition** is the stage in which the CS–UCS association is learned. Conditioning works best when the CS comes shortly before the UCS and predicts it (forward conditioning); presenting the UCS first (backward conditioning) produces little learning. Robert Rescorla showed that pairing alone is not enough. If the UCS is equally likely with or without the CS, little conditioning occurs, because the CS carries no predictive information.

**Extinction** is the weakening of the CR when the CS is repeatedly presented without the UCS. **Spontaneous recovery** is the reappearance of an extinguished CR after a rest period, usually in weaker form. Spontaneous recovery shows that extinction does not erase the original learning. It is new learning ("the tone now means no food") that suppresses the old association, which is why an extinguished fear can return later or in a new setting.

### Generalization and discrimination

**Stimulus generalization** is the tendency to give the CR to stimuli similar to the CS, with stronger responses to more similar stimuli. In the 1920 "Little Albert" study, John Watson and Rosalie Rayner conditioned an infant to fear a white rat by pairing it with a loud noise; the fear generalized to a rabbit and a fur coat. The study is now regarded as deeply unethical.

**Stimulus discrimination** is the learned ability to distinguish the CS from similar stimuli that do not predict the UCS. A dog fed after a high tone but never after a low tone learns to salivate only to the high tone.

### Second-order conditioning

In **second-order** (higher-order) **conditioning**, an established CS serves as the UCS for a new neutral stimulus. If a tone already produces salivation, a light presented before the tone can come to produce salivation too, even though the light was never paired with food. Second-order responses are usually weaker. The process helps explain why money or a brand logo can evoke emotion: they are linked to things that are linked to primary rewards.

### Biological preparedness and taste aversion

Early behaviorists assumed any stimulus could be conditioned to any response equally well. John Garcia showed otherwise. Rats that drank flavored water and were made ill hours later by radiation avoided that flavor afterward, often after a single pairing. Garcia and Robert Koelling then showed that rats readily associated a *taste* with illness and a light-and-noise cue with electric shock, but learned the reverse pairings poorly.

This is **biological preparedness**: animals are predisposed to learn associations that helped their ancestors survive. For a foraging animal, illness is far more likely to come from food than from a sound. Many people who became sick after a meal avoid that food for years, even knowing the illness had another cause.

<Callout type="high-yield" title="Why taste aversion is special">
Conditioned taste aversion breaks two usual rules: it can form after a **single pairing**, and it tolerates a **delay of hours** between the CS (taste) and the UCS (illness). Both reflect biological preparedness, which is selective: taste pairs easily with nausea, but lights and sounds do not.
</Callout>

## Operant Conditioning

In **operant conditioning**, an organism associates a voluntary behavior with its consequence. Behaviors followed by desirable outcomes become more likely; behaviors followed by undesirable outcomes become less likely.

### Thorndike and Skinner

Edward Thorndike placed hungry cats in "puzzle boxes" they could escape by pulling a loop or pressing a lever. Escapes were accidental at first, but over many trials useless behaviors dropped out and successful ones came sooner. Thorndike summarized this as the **law of effect**: behaviors followed by satisfying consequences are strengthened, and those followed by unpleasant consequences are weakened.

B. F. Skinner made the approach systematic. In an operant chamber (a "Skinner box"), a rat pressed a lever or a pigeon pecked a key, and the apparatus delivered consequences on a programmed schedule while recording every response.

### Reinforcement and punishment

Two questions define every operant consequence. Does the behavior become **more likely** (reinforcement) or **less likely** (punishment)? Is a stimulus **added** (positive) or **removed** (negative)? "Positive" and "negative" mean plus and minus, not good and bad.

| | Stimulus added (+) | Stimulus removed (−) |
|---|---|---|
| **Behavior increases** | **Positive reinforcement:** a child gets a sticker for cleaning her room | **Negative reinforcement:** ibuprofen relieves a headache, so you take it again next time |
| **Behavior decreases** | **Positive punishment:** a driver gets a speeding ticket | **Negative punishment:** a teenager loses phone privileges for missing curfew |

A seat-belt chime that stops when you buckle up is negative reinforcement: an unpleasant stimulus is removed, and buckling becomes more likely.

**Primary reinforcers** satisfy biological needs (food, water, relief from pain). **Secondary** (conditioned) **reinforcers** gain value through association with primary ones (money, grades, praise). A **token economy**, used in classrooms, psychiatric units and addiction treatment, awards tokens for desired behavior that can be exchanged for privileges.

Punishment suppresses behavior but has drawbacks: it does not teach an alternative, it can create fear of the punisher, and physical punishment models aggression. It works best when immediate, consistent, and paired with reinforcement of a better behavior.

<Callout type="trap" title="Negative reinforcement is not punishment">
This is the most common error in the chapter. Negative reinforcement *increases* a behavior by *removing* something unpleasant. Punishment *decreases* a behavior. Ask two separate questions: was something taken away, and did the behavior become more or less frequent?
</Callout>

### Schedules of reinforcement

Reinforcing every correct response is **continuous reinforcement**. It produces the fastest acquisition but also the fastest extinction, because when rewards stop the change is obvious. Reinforcing only some responses is **partial** (intermittent) **reinforcement**. Learning is slower, but the behavior is much more resistant to extinction, a finding called the partial reinforcement effect.

Partial schedules depend on whether reinforcement follows a **number of responses** (ratio) or the **passage of time** (interval), and whether the requirement is **fixed** or **variable**.

| Schedule | Rule | Response pattern | Resistance to extinction | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed ratio (FR) | After a set number of responses | High rate, brief pause after each reinforcer | Moderate | Paid for every 10 shirts sewn |
| Variable ratio (VR) | After an unpredictable number of responses | Highest, steady rate | Highest | Slot machines |
| Fixed interval (FI) | First response after a set time | "Scalloped": slow after reinforcement, accelerating as the time nears | Low to moderate | Studying mostly before a scheduled exam |
| Variable interval (VI) | First response after an unpredictable time | Slow, steady rate | High | Checking email or a social feed |

Two rules cover most questions. **Ratio schedules produce faster responding than interval schedules**, because more responses earn more reinforcers. **Variable schedules resist extinction better than fixed ones**, because when reinforcement is unpredictable it takes longer to notice it has stopped. Variable ratio therefore has both the highest response rate and the greatest resistance to extinction.

<Callout type="mnemonic" title="The gambler's schedule">
Slot machines pay on a **variable ratio**, which is why gamblers keep pulling after long losing streaks: the next pull might pay. "Ratio" means responses count; "interval" means the clock counts; "variable" means you can't predict it, so you can't tell when it has stopped.
</Callout>

### Shaping and instinctive drift

Complex behaviors rarely occur on their own, so they cannot simply be waited for and reinforced. In **shaping**, the trainer reinforces **successive approximations** to the target. To teach a rat to press a lever, reward it for facing the lever, then approaching it, then touching it, then pressing it.

Operant conditioning, like classical conditioning, has biological limits. Keller and Marian Breland, who trained animals commercially, found that raccoons taught to drop coins into a box began rubbing the coins together as they would food. This **instinctive drift**, a reversion of trained behavior toward instinctive patterns, is the operant counterpart of biological preparedness.

### Escape and avoidance learning

Both of these are maintained by **negative reinforcement**.

- In **escape learning**, the behavior *ends* an aversive stimulus already present. A rat crosses a shuttle box once the floor is electrified; you leave a stuffy room once a headache starts.
- In **avoidance learning**, the behavior *prevents* the aversive stimulus. If a warning light precedes the shock, the rat learns to cross when the light appears and is never shocked. Leaving early to beat rush-hour traffic is avoidance.

Avoidance is often learned in two stages: the warning signal comes to produce fear through classical conditioning, and the avoidance response is then negatively reinforced by fear reduction. Because a successful avoider never meets the aversive stimulus, it never learns that the danger may be gone, which helps explain why phobias persist.

### Learned helplessness

In experiments in the late 1960s, Martin Seligman and Steven Maier gave dogs electric shocks. Some dogs could stop the shocks by pressing a panel; others received identical shocks with no control over them. Later, in a shuttle box where they could escape shock by jumping a low barrier, dogs that had had control learned to escape quickly. Many dogs that had experienced uncontrollable shock passively endured it.

This is **learned helplessness**: after repeated uncontrollable aversive events, an organism stops trying to escape even when escape becomes possible. The critical variable was lack of *control*, since both groups received the same shocks. Learned helplessness became an influential model of depression.

## Observational and Cognitive Learning

### Observational learning and the Bobo doll

**Observational learning** (modeling) is learning by watching others. In Albert Bandura's Bobo doll studies of the early 1960s, preschoolers watched an adult either attack an inflatable clown doll, hitting it and shouting at it, or play quietly. Children who saw the aggressive model were far more likely to imitate the specific aggressive acts, often copying the model's words.

In a later version, children saw the model rewarded, punished or given no consequence. Those who saw the model punished imitated less. But when every child was then offered a reward for reproducing the model's behavior, the group differences largely disappeared. All groups had *learned* the behavior equally; the consequences they had observed affected only whether they chose to *perform* it. This learning–performance distinction is a favorite MCAT point.

Bandura described four processes required for observational learning: **attention** to the model, **retention** of what was observed, **reproduction** (the ability to perform it), and **motivation** to do so. Seeing a model rewarded is **vicarious reinforcement**; seeing a model punished is **vicarious punishment**. Models are imitated more when they are high in status, similar to the observer, or warm.

<Callout type="mnemonic" title="Bandura's four processes: ARRM">
**A**ttention, **R**etention, **R**eproduction, **M**otivation. A child who watches a skateboarder won't learn the trick if she wasn't watching, can't remember it, lacks the balance to do it, or has no reason to try.
</Callout>

### Mirror neurons

In the 1990s, Giacomo Rizzolatti's group recording from the premotor cortex of macaque monkeys found neurons that fired both when a monkey grasped an object and when it watched someone else grasp it. These **mirror neurons** have been proposed as a basis for imitation, for understanding others' actions and possibly for empathy, and imaging suggests comparable systems in human frontal and parietal cortex. How much they explain is still debated, so treat them as a plausible mechanism, not a settled one.

### Latent learning and cognitive maps

Strict behaviorists held that learning requires reinforcement. Edward Tolman challenged that view. In a study with Charles Honzik, three groups of rats ran a complex maze daily. One group always found food at the end, one never did, and one found no food for ten days and then began receiving it. The third group almost immediately performed as well as the group rewarded all along.

Those rats must have learned the maze during the unrewarded days without showing it. Tolman called this **latent learning**, learning that occurs without reinforcement and stays hidden until there is a reason to display it, and he proposed that the rats had formed a **cognitive map** of the maze. A related idea is **insight learning**, the sudden grasp of a solution, which Wolfgang Köhler described in chimpanzees that stacked boxes to reach hanging bananas.

## The Stages of Memory

The **multi-store model** of Richard Atkinson and Richard Shiffrin (1968) describes three stores: sensory memory, short-term memory and long-term memory. Moving through them requires **encoding** (getting information in), **storage** (keeping it) and **retrieval** (getting it out).

### Sensory memory

**Sensory memory** holds a brief, high-capacity, literal copy of sensory input, long enough for attention to select what matters.

**Iconic memory**, the visual form, lasts a fraction of a second. George Sperling demonstrated it in 1960 by flashing a grid of letters for about a twentieth of a second. Asked to report the whole grid, participants recalled only about four letters. But when a tone right after the flash signaled which row to report, they could report almost any row accurately, so nearly the whole grid must briefly have been available. Delaying the tone by about a second wiped out this advantage, showing how fast the icon fades.

**Echoic memory**, the auditory form, lasts longer, roughly three or four seconds, which is why you can "replay" something you just heard but weren't attending to.

### Short-term memory and working memory

Attended information enters **short-term memory (STM)**, which lasts roughly 15 to 30 seconds without rehearsal. George Miller famously put its capacity at "seven plus or minus two" items; more recent estimates are closer to four meaningful units. **Chunking**, grouping items into meaningful units, stretches that capacity: F B I C I A N F L is nine letters but three chunks. **Maintenance rehearsal**, simple repetition, keeps information in STM but transfers it to long-term memory poorly.

Alan Baddeley and Graham Hitch argued in 1974 that STM is better described as **working memory**, an active workspace for manipulating information.

- The **central executive** directs attention and coordinates the other components. It is closely tied to the prefrontal cortex.
- The **phonological loop** holds verbal and auditory information, refreshed by subvocal rehearsal.
- The **visuospatial sketchpad** holds visual images and spatial layouts.
- The **episodic buffer**, added in 2000, integrates information from the other components and long-term memory into unified episodes.

The evidence for separate components comes from **dual-task** experiments. Two verbal tasks performed together interfere heavily, as do two visual tasks, but a verbal task and a visual task interfere much less.

<Callout type="high-yield" title="Matching tasks to Baddeley's components">

- Repeating a phone number under your breath: **phonological loop**
- Mentally rotating a shape or picturing a route: **visuospatial sketchpad**
- Switching tasks, ignoring distractions, planning: **central executive**
- Binding sights, sounds and prior knowledge into one scene: **episodic buffer**

</Callout>

### Long-term memory: explicit and implicit

**Long-term memory (LTM)** has essentially unlimited capacity and duration. Its divisions depend on whether retrieval is conscious.

**Explicit** (declarative) **memory** can be consciously recalled. Endel Tulving distinguished **episodic memory**, for personal experiences tied to a time and place, from **semantic memory**, for general facts and knowledge.

**Implicit** (nondeclarative) **memory** affects behavior without conscious recollection. It includes **procedural memory** for skills and habits, **priming**, and **classically conditioned responses**.

The strongest evidence that these are separate systems comes from amnesic patients who learn new skills with no memory of having practiced, described at the end of this chapter.

## Encoding: Getting Information In

**Automatic processing** captures information such as time, place and frequency with little effort. Most studying requires **effortful processing**, and several factors determine how well it works.

### Levels of processing

Fergus Craik and Robert Lockhart proposed in 1972 that memory depends on **depth of processing**. **Structural** (shallow) processing attends to appearance ("Is the word in capitals?"), **phonemic** processing to sound ("Does it rhyme with *train*?"), and **semantic** (deep) processing to meaning ("Does it fit the sentence *She put the ___ in the oven*?"). On a surprise memory test after these orienting questions, recall is best for semantically processed words and worst for structurally processed ones. **Elaborative rehearsal**, linking new information to what you already know, is deep processing and far more effective than maintenance rehearsal.

### The self-reference effect

People remember words better when they judge whether each describes *them* ("Does *generous* describe you?") than when they judge the word's general meaning. This **self-reference effect** works because the self is a rich, well-organized structure in memory, so linking information to it creates many retrieval routes.

### The spacing effect and retrieval practice

The **spacing effect** is the finding that information is retained better when study is spread over time (**distributed practice**) than crammed together (**massed practice**). Ebbinghaus observed it in the 1880s. Cramming can carry you through the next morning's test, but most of the material is soon lost. Closely related is the **testing effect**: retrieving information, as with practice questions or flashcards, strengthens memory more than rereading. Other aids include **dual coding** (storing information both verbally and visually) and **mnemonic devices** such as acronyms and the **method of loci**, which places items along an imagined familiar route.

<Callout type="note" title="This site is built on these findings">
The quizzes and flashcards are retrieval practice, and reviewing them across several days is distributed practice. Rereading feels productive because material becomes familiar, but familiarity is a weak cue for recall on test day.
</Callout>

## Retrieval: Getting Information Out

Many apparent memory failures are retrieval failures: the information is stored, but the right cue is missing.

### Recall, recognition and relearning

- **Recall** means retrieving information with few or no cues, as in an essay question. Cued recall provides partial hints.
- **Recognition** means identifying learned information among options, as in multiple choice. It is easier because the correct answer acts as its own cue.
- **Relearning** measures how much faster material is learned a second time. The time saved, which Ebbinghaus called **savings**, can reveal memories that neither recall nor recognition detects.

### Context, state and mood

The **encoding specificity principle** states that a retrieval cue works to the extent that it matches information present at encoding.

**Context-dependent memory** is better recall when the external environment at retrieval matches the one at encoding. In a 1975 study by Duncan Godden and Alan Baddeley, divers learned word lists on land or underwater and were tested in the same or the other environment. Recall was substantially better when the environments matched.

**State-dependent memory** is better recall when your *internal* state matches, for instance being intoxicated or caffeinated at both encoding and retrieval. **Mood-congruent memory** is the tendency to recall memories whose emotional tone matches your current mood, so a depressed person more readily recalls failures, which can deepen the depression.

<Callout type="trap" title="Context versus state">
*Context*-dependent memory involves the **external** environment (a room, underwater, background music). *State*-dependent memory involves the **internal** physiological state (alcohol, drugs, arousal). Mood-congruent memory concerns the *emotional content* of what is recalled.
</Callout>

### The serial position effect

When people study a list and recall it in any order, they remember the first and last items best, producing a U-shaped curve. The **primacy effect** arises because early items get more rehearsal and reach long-term memory. The **recency effect** arises because the last items are still in short-term memory at recall.

The two can be separated. Delaying recall by about 30 seconds of a distracting task, such as counting backward by threes, eliminates the recency effect because STM has been cleared, while primacy remains. Presenting items very quickly, leaving little time for rehearsal, reduces primacy but leaves recency intact.

### Priming and semantic networks

**Priming** is the activation, often unconscious, of associations in memory. After seeing *nurse*, people recognize *doctor* faster than *bread*. One account is that semantic memory forms a **semantic network** of linked concepts, and activating one concept spreads activation to its neighbors (**spreading activation**). Because priming is implicit, amnesic patients who cannot recall seeing a word list still show priming for its words. The **tip-of-the-tongue** state, knowing a word's first letter but not the word, is a vivid example of partial retrieval.

## Forgetting and Memory Errors

### The Ebbinghaus forgetting curve

In the 1880s, Hermann Ebbinghaus memorized lists of nonsense syllables (like *DAX* or *ZOF*), chosen to minimize prior associations, and tested himself after various delays. His **forgetting curve** showed rapid loss at first, with much of the material gone within a day, followed by a slower decline that levels off. What survives the first few days tends to last.

### Why we forget

- **Encoding failure:** the information never reached LTM. Most people cannot draw a coin they have handled thousands of times.
- **Decay:** traces fade with disuse. Decay clearly operates in sensory memory and STM but is hard to demonstrate in LTM.
- **Interference:** other information disrupts memory. In **proactive interference**, *old* information disrupts *new*, as when you keep writing last year's date in January. In **retroactive interference**, *new* information disrupts *old*, as when learning a new password makes the previous one hard to recall.
- **Retrieval failure:** the memory is stored, but the cue is missing.

<Callout type="mnemonic" title="Pro and retro">
**Pro**active works **for**ward: the old memory reaches ahead and disrupts the new one. **Retro**active works **back**ward: the new memory reaches back and disrupts the old one. Name the interference by the direction it travels.
</Callout>

### Memory as reconstruction

Memory is not a recording. Each retrieval rebuilds the memory from fragments, general knowledge and expectation, and the rebuilt version can be stored again with changes. In 1932, Frederic Bartlett showed that people retelling an unfamiliar folk tale reshaped it over time to fit their own cultural **schemas**.

The **misinformation effect** is the incorporation of misleading post-event information into memory. In Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer's 1974 study, participants watched film of a car accident and were asked how fast the cars were going when they "hit" or "smashed into" each other. The "smashed" group gave higher speed estimates and, a week later, was more likely to report seeing broken glass, though there was none. Later studies led people to "remember" whole events that never happened, such as being lost in a mall as a child.

A **source monitoring error** occurs when you remember information but misattribute its source: you recall a news story but think a friend told you, or you "remember" a childhood event you actually saw in a photograph. Many misinformation effects are source errors.

Other reconstructive errors include false recall of a word never presented (after studying *bed, rest, tired, dream*, many people "remember" *sleep*), **imagination inflation**, and **flashbulb memories**: vivid memories of emotionally significant events that feel highly accurate but change over time much like ordinary memories. Confidence and vividness are not reliable signs of accuracy, which matters greatly for eyewitness testimony.

## The Neuroscience of Memory, Aging, and Dementia

### Brain structures for different kinds of memory

In 1953, a patient known for decades as H.M. (Henry Molaison) had much of his medial temporal lobes removed on both sides, including much of the hippocampus, to control severe epilepsy. He was left with profound **anterograde amnesia**: he could no longer form new explicit long-term memories, though older memories were largely intact and his working memory let him hold a conversation. Yet over several days of practicing mirror drawing, his performance improved steadily even though he denied ever having done the task. His case showed that the hippocampus is needed to form new explicit memories but not implicit ones.

| Structure | Role in memory |
|---|---|
| Hippocampus and medial temporal lobe | Consolidating new explicit memories; spatial memory |
| Cerebral cortex | Long-term storage of explicit memories |
| Prefrontal cortex | Working memory; strategic retrieval |
| Amygdala | Emotional, especially fear-related, memories |
| Cerebellum | Motor skills; conditioned motor responses such as eyeblink conditioning |
| Basal ganglia | Procedural memory and habits |

The hippocampus is not the permanent store. Through **consolidation**, aided by sleep, explicit memories gradually become independent of it, which is why hippocampal damage spares old memories more than recent ones. Emotional arousal strengthens memory partly through stress hormones such as epinephrine and cortisol, which increase amygdala activity and enhance hippocampal consolidation.

### Types of amnesia

- **Anterograde amnesia:** inability to form new memories after the onset of damage; typical of hippocampal injury.
- **Retrograde amnesia:** loss of memories formed before the onset. It is often temporally graded, with recent memories lost and older ones spared, so a blow to the head commonly erases the events just before it.
- **Korsakoff syndrome:** caused by severe thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency, usually from chronic alcohol use. It damages the mammillary bodies and thalamus and causes severe anterograde amnesia, some retrograde amnesia, and **confabulation**, the production of false memories without intent to deceive.
- **Infantile amnesia:** the normal inability to recall events from roughly the first three or four years of life.

### Long-term potentiation

In 1949, Donald Hebb proposed that when one neuron repeatedly helps fire another, the connection between them strengthens, an idea later summarized as "neurons that fire together wire together." In 1973, Timothy Bliss and Terje Lømo found that brief, high-frequency stimulation of a pathway in the rabbit hippocampus produced a long-lasting increase in synaptic strength. This **long-term potentiation (LTP)** is the leading cellular model of memory.

At many hippocampal synapses, LTP depends on two kinds of glutamate receptor. **AMPA receptors** open when glutamate binds and admit sodium, producing ordinary excitatory postsynaptic potentials. **NMDA receptors** need two conditions at once: glutamate must bind, *and* the postsynaptic membrane must already be depolarized to expel a magnesium ion that blocks the channel. Only then does calcium flow in. The NMDA receptor is therefore a **coincidence detector**, opening only when presynaptic and postsynaptic neurons are active together, exactly as Hebb's rule requires.

Calcium entry activates protein kinases that insert more AMPA receptors into the postsynaptic membrane, making the synapse more responsive. Early LTP lasts hours and modifies existing proteins; late LTP requires gene expression, new protein synthesis and structural growth. Blocking NMDA receptors in rodents impairs both LTP and spatial learning. The reverse process, **long-term depression**, weakens synapses, and the brain's overall capacity to change with experience is **neuroplasticity**.

<Callout type="connection" title="Glutamate and the NMDA receptor" to="/bb/amino-acids-and-proteins">
Glutamate, the brain's main excitatory neurotransmitter, is the anionic form of the amino acid glutamic acid at physiological pH. The AMPA and NMDA receptors that mediate LTP are ligand-gated ion channels assembled from protein subunits. At the molecular level, memory is a story about amino acids and proteins.
</Callout>

### Memory and normal aging

Aging affects memory selectively.

- **Working memory** and **processing speed** decline steadily from early adulthood.
- **Episodic memory** declines, especially **free recall**, while **recognition** is much better preserved. An older adult may struggle to recall a new acquaintance's name but pick it out of a list easily.
- **Source memory** declines, increasing vulnerability to source errors.
- **Prospective memory** declines most for **time-based** tasks ("take the pill at 3 p.m.") and less for **event-based** tasks ("take it with dinner").
- **Semantic memory**, vocabulary and **procedural memory** are maintained and often keep growing.

This parallels the decline of **fluid intelligence** (reasoning quickly about new problems) alongside stable or rising **crystallized intelligence** (accumulated knowledge).

### Dementia

**Dementia** is a decline in memory and other cognitive abilities severe enough to impair daily functioning, and it is not a normal part of aging. It differs from **delirium**, an acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention that is often caused by infection or medication and is often reversible.

**Alzheimer's disease**, the most common cause, is marked by extracellular **amyloid plaques** of beta-amyloid and intracellular **neurofibrillary tangles** of tau protein. Damage typically begins in the entorhinal cortex and hippocampus, so difficulty forming new memories is usually the first symptom, while older memories persist longer. Later it affects language, spatial ability, judgment and personality. Alzheimer's also destroys acetylcholine-producing neurons in the basal forebrain, and several of its medications are acetylcholinesterase inhibitors. The strongest genetic risk factor for the common late-onset form is the APOE ε4 allele.

<Callout type="connection" title="Treating dementia with enzyme inhibitors" to="/bb/enzymes-and-kinetics">
Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors block the enzyme that hydrolyzes acetylcholine, prolonging its action at cholinergic synapses. They ease symptoms without stopping the underlying degeneration.
</Callout>

Other dementias include **vascular dementia**, caused by strokes or reduced blood flow and often progressing in steps; **dementia with Lewy bodies**, with fluctuating attention, visual hallucinations and parkinsonian movement symptoms; and **frontotemporal dementia**, which often starts earlier in life with changes in personality, behavior or language before memory is affected.

Learning and memory run through the entire P/S section. Conditioning underlies models of addiction, anxiety and depression; observational learning shapes socialization and attitudes; and reconstructive memory shapes how we see ourselves and others. When a later chapter asks why a behavior persists or how a belief formed, the mechanisms here are usually part of the answer.
